## A first meeting {#a-first-meeting role=orient objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

It is Monday morning at a small language school in Bergen. Two new students arrive early and wait outside their classroom.

::: {.reading translation="Hi! My name is Mina. What’s your name?" dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Hei! Jeg heter Mina. Hva heter du?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Hi, Mina. My name is Jonas." dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Hei, Mina. Jeg heter Jonas.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Nice to meet you." dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Hyggelig å møte deg.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Nice to meet you too." dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Hyggelig å møte deg også.
:::

The classroom door opens.

::: {.reading translation="See you!" dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Vi ses!
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, see you. Bye!" dialogue_id="first-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, vi ses. Ha det!
:::

Mina and Jonas complete a simple first-meeting exchange. Each part has a clear job: they greet each other, give one identity fact—their name—ask for the same information, and close the conversation.

## The shape of the exchange {#the-shape-of-the-exchange role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

A first meeting can be short and still feel complete:

::: examples
- no: Hei!
- en: Hi!
- no: Jeg heter Mina.
- en: My name is Mina.
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
- no: Jeg heter Jonas.
- en: My name is Jonas.
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg.
- en: Nice to meet you.
- no: Ha det!
- en: Bye!
:::

You do not need a long introduction. At this stage, the important skill is keeping the exchange moving from opening to closing.

Read one line from the conversation: Mina says «Jeg heter Mina.» Check what this line means in the exchange.

## Greeting with *hei* {#greeting-with-hei role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

The most useful general greeting is:

::: examples
- no: Hei!
- en: Hi! / Hello!
:::

It works in many everyday situations. You can use it when meeting someone for the first time, entering a small shop, or greeting someone you already know.

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Nora.
- en: Hi! My name is Nora.
- no: Hei, Nora.
- en: Hi, Nora.
:::

Norwegian often uses a person’s name directly after the greeting:

::: examples
- no: Hei, Amir!
- en: Hi, Amir!
:::

The greeting can stand alone, but in a first meeting it naturally leads into your identity fact:

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Lea.
- en: Hi! My name is Lea.
:::

## Saying your name with *jeg heter* {#saying-your-name-with-jeg-heter role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

To state your name, use:

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter + name.
- en: My name is + name.
:::

Literally, **jeg heter** is closer to “I am called,” but the natural English translation is “My name is.”

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Sara.
- en: My name is Sara.
- no: Jeg heter Daniel.
- en: My name is Daniel.
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Lin.
- en: Hi! My name is Lin.
:::

Keep **jeg heter** together as the useful expression. You do not need to analyze or change it to introduce yourself.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Eva.
- en: My name is Eva.
- no: Hun heter Eva.
- en: Her name is Eva.
:::

For this first-meeting exchange, the form you need for yourself is **jeg heter**.

Norwegian normally does not use an equivalent of English “name” in this sentence.

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Emil.
- en: My name is Emil.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg navn Emil.
- en: Incorrect: This does not form a natural Norwegian sentence.
:::

Another common mistake is to omit **jeg**:

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Amina.
- en: My name is Amina.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Heter Amina.
- en: Incorrect: This does not form a natural Norwegian sentence.
:::

Norwegian normally states the subject **jeg** here.

## Asking the other person’s name {#asking-the-other-persons-name role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

After giving your name, invite the other person to do the same:

::: examples
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
:::

The question begins with **hva**, meaning “what.” For now, learn the complete question as one useful expression.

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Oskar. Hva heter du?
- en: My name is Oskar. What’s your name?
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Fatima. Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! My name is Fatima. What’s your name?
:::

Notice the contrast:

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Mina.
- en: My name is Mina.
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
:::

In the statement, **jeg** means “I.” In the question, **du** means “you.”

A frequent error is to copy English word order too directly:

::: examples
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Hva du heter?
- en: Incorrect: This is not the correct first-meeting question.
:::

The correct first-meeting question is **Hva heter du?**

Another possible mistake is asking the question but never giving your own name. Compare these openings:

::: examples
- no: Hei! Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! What’s your name?
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Ada. Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! My name is Ada. What’s your name?
:::

Both can be understood, but the second feels more balanced in a first meeting because you offer the same information that you request.

## Responding to the name question {#responding-to-the-name-question role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

The answer uses the same form you used for your own introduction:

::: examples
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
- no: Jeg heter Sigrid.
- en: My name is Sigrid.
:::

The speakers can also acknowledge each other’s names:

::: examples
- no: Hei, Sigrid.
- en: Hi, Sigrid.
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg, Sigrid.
- en: Nice to meet you, Sigrid.
:::

Using the person’s name is optional, but it can make the response feel attentive and friendly.

Here is another complete opening:

::: {.reading translation="Hi! My name is Theo. What’s your name?" dialogue_id="complete-opening" speaker_id="theo" speaker_name="Theo"}
Hei! Jeg heter Theo. Hva heter du?
:::

::: {.reading translation="My name is Iben." dialogue_id="complete-opening" speaker_id="iben" speaker_name="Iben"}
Jeg heter Iben.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Hi, Iben. Nice to meet you." dialogue_id="complete-opening" speaker_id="theo" speaker_name="Theo"}
Hei, Iben. Hyggelig å møte deg.
:::

The response **Jeg heter Iben** gives exactly the information requested. It does not need to become a longer personal profile.

## Marking the meeting as friendly {#marking-the-meeting-as-friendly role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

After names have been exchanged, a common expression is:

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg.
- en: Nice to meet you.
:::

You can respond with the same expression:

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg.
- en: Nice to meet you.
:::

You can also add **også**, meaning “too” or “also”:

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg også.
- en: Nice to meet you too.
:::

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg.
- en: Nice to meet you.
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg også.
- en: Nice to meet you too.
:::

The second version explicitly returns the friendly comment.

Do not translate English word for word. Use the complete Norwegian expression:

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg.
- en: Nice to meet you.
:::

A learner may be tempted to build a sentence around English “nice,” but **Hyggelig å møte deg** is the natural phrase to remember.

## Closing the exchange {#closing-the-exchange role=model objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

A first meeting needs an ending. Two useful closing expressions are:

::: examples
- no: Ha det!
- en: Bye!
- no: Vi ses!
- en: See you!
:::

**Ha det** is a common general goodbye. **Vi ses** is natural when you expect to see the person again.

At the language school, Mina and Jonas are about to enter the same classroom, so **Vi ses** fits well.

::: {.reading translation="See you!" dialogue_id="school-closing" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Vi ses!
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, see you. Bye!" dialogue_id="school-closing" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, vi ses. Ha det!
:::

You can use either closing by itself:

::: examples
- no: Ha det!
- en: Bye!
- no: Vi ses!
- en: See you!
:::

You can also use both:

::: examples
- no: Vi ses. Ha det!
- en: See you. Bye!
:::

The closing should come after the introduction has reached a natural stopping point.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Kim. Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! My name is Kim. What’s your name?
:::

This opens the exchange.

::: examples
- no: Hyggelig å møte deg. Ha det!
- en: Nice to meet you. Bye!
:::

This prepares to close it.

:::

::: {.reading translation="Hi! My name is Isak." dialogue_id="complete-first-meeting" speaker_id="isak" speaker_name="Isak"}
Hei! Jeg heter Isak.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Nice to meet you." dialogue_id="complete-first-meeting" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}

:::

::: {.reading translation="Nice to meet you too." dialogue_id="complete-first-meeting" speaker_id="isak" speaker_name="Isak"}
Hyggelig å møte deg også.
:::

::: {.reading translation="See you!" dialogue_id="complete-first-meeting" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}

The exchange is complete because both speakers know the other person’s name and the conversation has a clear closing.

## Common problems in the complete exchange {#common-problems-in-the-complete-exchange role=contrast objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

**Giving a name but not inviting a response**

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Pia.
- en: Hi! My name is Pia.
:::

This is understandable, but it may leave the other person unsure whether to continue.

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Pia. Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! My name is Pia. What’s your name?
:::

This clearly invites the other person into the exchange.

**Asking without answering**

::: {.reading translation="What’s your name?" dialogue_id="asking-without-answering" speaker_id="a" speaker_name="A"}
Hva heter du?
:::

::: {.reading translation="What’s your name?" dialogue_id="asking-without-answering" speaker_id="b" speaker_name="B"}
Hva heter du?
:::

Speaker B has not answered. A natural response gives the requested identity fact:

::: {.reading translation="My name is Mats. What’s your name?" dialogue_id="asking-without-answering" speaker_id="b" speaker_name="B"}
Jeg heter Mats. Hva heter du?
:::

**Mixing up *jeg* and *du***

::: examples
- no: Jeg heter Runa.
- en: My name is Runa.
- no: Hva heter du?
- en: What’s your name?
:::

Use **jeg** when speaking about yourself and **du** when addressing the other person.

**Ending too suddenly**

::: {.reading translation="My name is Eline." dialogue_id="ending-too-suddenly" speaker_id="a" speaker_name="A"}
Jeg heter Eline.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Bye!" dialogue_id="ending-too-suddenly" speaker_id="b" speaker_name="B"}
Ha det!
:::

The goodbye is grammatical, but the exchange feels abrupt. A friendly acknowledgment creates a smoother close:

::: {.reading translation="Nice to meet you. Bye!" dialogue_id="ending-too-suddenly" speaker_id="b" speaker_name="B"}
Hyggelig å møte deg. Ha det!
:::

## Guided exchange practice {#guided-exchange-practice role=recap objectives=obj-greet-and-introduce-yourself-01}

Return to Mina and Jonas outside the classroom. Retell their meeting in your own words while keeping the same four communicative jobs. Use different names, but do not add extra identity information.

Your exchange should include a greeting, one speaker’s name, a question for the other person’s name, the answer, and a friendly closing.

{{exercise: first_day_exchange}}

Next, imagine that you meet someone at a community event. Begin as the first speaker and produce a short complete exchange for both people.

Your response should sound like one connected conversation, not isolated phrases, with both speakers responding appropriately to what the other has said.

Finally, imagine that you are the second speaker. Someone says:

::: examples
- no: Hei! Jeg heter Aleksander. Hva heter du?
- en: Hi! My name is Aleksander. What’s your name?
:::

Continue and finish the meeting. Give your name, return a friendly meeting expression, and close.

Your response should answer Aleksander’s question and bring the conversation to a natural end.

They no longer need to ask each other’s names. Their first exchange worked: each greeted the other, shared a name, asked for the same information, and closed clearly. Now they can recognize and greet each other by name.
