You already know how these five verbs look. The B2 skill is choosing the right one, fast, while a real conversation is moving. This chapter follows one weekend plan from invitation to aftermath, and trains the small decisions that make the difference.

## A weekend in Telemark {#a-weekend-in-telemark role=orient objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

It is Thursday. Colleagues Ingrid and Thomas meet in the office kitchen with their coffee.

::: {.reading translation="Soon it's the weekend again. Do you have any plans, Thomas?" dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Nå er det snart helg igjen. Har du noen planer, Thomas?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I'm going to the family cabin in Telemark. You can come along, if you like." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Jeg skal til familiehytta i Telemark. Du kan bli med, hvis du vil.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I'd really like that! I want to get out of the city." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Det vil jeg gjerne! Jeg har lyst til å komme meg ut av byen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Lovely! But we're leaving already at ten on Friday, so you have to take that day off." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Koselig! Men vi reiser allerede klokka ti på fredag, så du må ta deg fri den dagen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That will be fine. I have enough holiday days left to take." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Det skal gå greit. Jeg har nok feriedager til gode.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Can you bring your car, or should we take the train?" dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Kan du ta med bilen din, eller skal vi ta toget?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I can drive. Then we can split the petrol." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Jeg kan kjøre. Så kan vi dele på bensinen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Fine. And you should bring proper boots — the path up to the cabin is rather muddy right now." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Greit. Og du bør ta med ordentlige støvler — stien opp til hytta er ganske gjørmete nå.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good thing I don't own any... Then I have to go out and buy a pair tonight." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Godt at jeg ikke har noen... Da må jeg ut og kjøpe et par i kveld.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Just remember that you mustn't wear shoes inside the cabin. That's a house rule of ours." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Husk bare at man må ikke gå med sko inne i hytta. Det er husregel hos oss.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good to know! What about food — what am I supposed to bring?" dialogue_id=office speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Godt å vite! Hva med mat — hva skal jeg ta med?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Bring whatever you want. But it will surely get cold up there at night, so pack something warm." dialogue_id=office speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Ta med det du vil ha. Men det vil sikkert bli kaldt der oppe om natten, så pakk noe varmt.
:::

**What is happening**

Thomas is going to his family's cabin and invites Ingrid. The catch: they leave at ten on Friday, so Ingrid has to take the day off — which she can, since she has holiday days to spare. She drives, they split the petrol, and Thomas passes on some practical advice about boots, a house rule about shoes, and a warning about cold nights. Notice how much of this negotiation runs through five tiny verbs: *må, kan, skal, vil, bør*.

**Useful expressions to take with you**

::: examples
- no: Det skal gå greit.
- en: It will be fine. (said to reassure someone)
- no: Det vil jeg gjerne!
- en: I'd really like to!
- no: Godt å vite!
- en: Good to know!
- no: å ta seg fri
- en: to take time off work
- no: feriedager til gode
- en: holiday days still left to take
- no: husregel
- en: house rule
:::

## Five verbs, five kinds of pressure {#five-verbs-five-kinds-of-pressure role=model objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

In Norwegian these verbs are called modal verbs (modale hjelpeverb): small verbs that stand in front of another verb's infinitive (infinitiv) and say something about that action — how necessary it is, whether it is possible, whether it is planned, wanted, or advisable.

Two facts you already know are worth repeating, because they hold for all five:

- The infinitive after a modal has no *å*: *jeg må gå*, never *jeg må å gå*. This is a very common writing mistake at this level.
- Ordinary present-tense verbs add *-r* — *reiser, snakker* — but these modals do not (*bør* simply ends in *-r* as part of the word).

All five also have past tense (preteritum) forms — *måtte, kunne, skulle, ville, burde* — but this chapter trains the present-tense choice, because that is where meaning does the daily work.

From the dialogue alone, you can already hear the five jobs:

- *må* — necessity: Du må ta deg fri den dagen. / You have to take that day off.
- *kan* — ability and possibility: Jeg kan kjøre. / I can drive.
- *skal* — plan or promise: Jeg skal til familiehytta. / I'm going to the family cabin.
- *vil* — wish, and sometimes prediction: Det vil jeg gjerne! / I'd really like to! — and Det vil sikkert bli kaldt. / It will surely get cold.
- *bør* — advice: Du bør ta med ordentlige støvler. / You should bring proper boots.

## Necessity and its limits: må {#necessity-and-its-limits role=contrast objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

Use *må* when something outside the speaker makes the action necessary. The pressure can come from a deadline, a practical situation, or a rule. In the dialogue it appears three times, each with a different source:

- Thomas: *du må ta deg fri* — necessity from the departure time.
- Ingrid: *da må jeg ut og kjøpe et par* — necessity created by her lack of boots.
- Thomas: *man må ikke gå med sko inne i hytta* — necessity's sharp edge: a rule that forbids.

That third use is where English-speaking learners stumble. *Må ikke* does **not** mean *don't have to*. It means *must not* — the action is forbidden:

::: examples
- no: Du må ikke ta med hund til hytta.
- en: You must not bring a dog to the cabin. (it is against the rules)
- no: Du trenger ikke ta med sengetøy.
- en: You don't need to bring bedding. (there is bedding there already)
:::

When an obligation disappears, Norwegian switches verbs to *trenger ikke* (or *behøver ikke*). When the obligation flips into a ban, it stays with *må* and adds *ikke*.

**Rule of thumb:** *må* = necessary, *må ikke* = forbidden, *trenger ikke* = not necessary.

## The open door: kan {#the-open-door-kan role=model objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

*Kan* is the lowest-pressure modal. It presents an action as possible, as within someone's ability, or as permitted — the door is open, but nobody is pushing you through it. In the dialogue it does all three jobs:

- Ability: *Jeg kan kjøre.* / I can drive.
- Offer and permission: *Du kan bli med, hvis du vil.* / You can come along, if you like.
- Request: *Kan du ta med bilen din?* / Can you bring your car?

Compare *kan* and *må* on the same action and feel the pressure change:

::: examples
- no: Du kan ta deg fri på fredag.
- en: You can take Friday off. (it is possible; up to you)
- no: Du må ta deg fri på fredag.
- en: You have to take Friday off. (necessary)
:::

To grant a request warmly, Norwegians often add *gjerne*:

::: examples
- no: Kan jeg låne sykkelen din i morgen?
- en: Can I borrow your bike tomorrow?
- no: Ja, du kan gjerne låne den.
- en: Yes, feel free to borrow it. (du må gjerne is an even warmer please do)
:::

One fine distinction: *kan ikke* and *må ikke* can both stop an action, but they usually point at different things. *Må ikke* signals an explicit rule; *kan ikke* typically presents the action as unavailable, and is also used informally for things that are not allowed.

::: examples
- no: Du kan ikke parkere her.
- en: You can't park here. (there is no way / it is not allowed)
- no: Du må ikke parkere her.
- en: You must not park here. (a rule says no)
:::

**Rule of thumb:** *kan* opens an option; it does not itself mark necessity.

## Decided or desired: skal and vil {#decided-or-desired-skal-and-vil role=contrast objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

In English, *will* covers a lot of ground. Norwegian splits that ground between two verbs, and the split follows a simple question: is this decided, or is this just wanted or guessed?

*Skal* marks plans, arrangements, and promises — things on the calendar, things settled:

- *Jeg skal til familiehytta.* / I'm going to the family cabin. (it is arranged)
- *Det skal gå greit.* / That will be fine. (a promise, a reassurance — the speaker commits)
- *Hva skal jeg ta med?* / What am I supposed to bring? (asking what the plan assigns to me)

*Vil* marks wishes and predictions — things in someone's head:

- *Det vil jeg gjerne!* / I'd really like that!
- *Jeg vil gjerne ut av byen.* / I'd really like to get out of the city.
- *Det vil sikkert bli kaldt om natten.* / It will surely get cold at night. (a prediction, not a plan — note the marker *sikkert*, *nok*, or *kanskje* that usually travels with this use)

Watch the meaning turn when only the modal changes:

::: examples
- no: Vi skal flytte til Bergen i august.
- en: We are moving to Bergen in August. (decided, arranged)
- no: Vi vil flytte til Bergen i august.
- en: We want to move to Bergen in August. (a wish)
- no: Hva skal du gjøre i sommer?
- en: What are you going to do this summer? (asking about plans)
- no: Hva vil du gjøre i sommer?
- en: What do you want to do this summer? (asking about wishes)
:::

If you have compared *skulle* and *ville* before, this is the same line drawn more firmly: *skal* is the world as organized, *vil* is the world as wanted or expected.

**Rule of thumb:** *skal* when it is decided or promised; *vil* when it is wanted or guessed.

## Advice in between: bør {#advice-in-between role=model objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

*Bør* lives between the open door and the locked one. It recommends: the speaker thinks the action is wise, but leaves the choice with you. That is exactly what Thomas does with the boots — *du bør ta med ordentlige støvler* — advice from experience, not a rule.

Put the three pressures on one line:

::: examples
- no: Du kan ta med regnjakke.
- en: You can bring a rain jacket. (an option)
- no: Du bør ta med regnjakke.
- en: You should bring a rain jacket. (advisable)
- no: Du må ta med regnjakke.
- en: You have to bring a rain jacket. (required)
:::

The common mistake is translating *bør* as *must*. If Thomas had said *du må ta med støvler*, the friendly tip becomes a condition — as if the boots were required equipment. *Bør* never compels.

You will also hear *burde*, the preteritum form, doing the same advising job a shade more softly. Treat them as cousins: recognize both, use *bør* when in doubt.

**Rule of thumb:** use *bør* to recommend, not to require.

## One sentence, five pressures {#one-sentence-five-pressures role=contrast objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

To see the whole system at once, keep the sentence identical and change only the modal:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan møte deg klokka fire.
- en: I can meet you at four. (it is possible)
- no: Jeg må møte deg klokka fire.
- en: I have to meet you at four. (it is necessary)
- no: Jeg skal møte deg klokka fire.
- en: I am to meet you at four. (it is arranged)
- no: Jeg vil møte deg klokka fire.
- en: I want to meet you at four. (I wish to)
- no: Jeg bør møte deg klokka fire.
- en: I ought to meet you at four. (it would be wise)
:::

Same grammar, same clock — five different relationships between the speaker and the action. Choosing the modal is choosing that relationship.

## Sunday evening {#sunday-evening role=model objectives=obj-modal-verbs-in-context-01}

The trip is over. The same two people, now tired and happy, exchange messages — and the five verbs come along, doing new jobs.

::: {.reading translation="Home again. How are your legs doing?" dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Hjemme igjen. Hvordan går det med beina?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Stiff! But I want to get back to the cabin as soon as I can." dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Stive! Men jeg vil tilbake til hytta så fort jeg kan.
:::

::: {.reading translation="You can borrow the key during the winter holiday too, if you like." dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Du kan få låne nøkkelen i vinterferien også, hvis du vil.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then I have to learn how to light the wood stove first." dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Da må jeg lære meg å tenne vedovnen først.
:::

::: {.reading translation="You don't need to — the neighbour lights it for us every Friday." dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Det trenger du ikke — naboen fyrer opp for oss hver fredag.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good to know. Shall we set dates for Easter already now?" dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name=Ingrid}
Godt å vite. Skal vi bestemme datoer for påsken allerede nå?
:::

::: {.reading translation="We should probably wait until January, but we can certainly start dreaming." dialogue_id=sunday speaker_id=thomas speaker_name=Thomas}
Vi bør nok vente til januar, men vi kan godt begynne å drømme.
:::

Notice how the system from this chapter keeps paying off: Ingrid's *må* about the stove is answered with *trenger du ikke*, her *skal vi* turns a plan into a shared proposal, and Thomas closes with *bør* and *kan* placed exactly where advice and possibility belong.
