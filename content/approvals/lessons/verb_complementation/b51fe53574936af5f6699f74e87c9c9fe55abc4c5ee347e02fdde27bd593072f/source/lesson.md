## A weekend that depends on a deadline {#weekend-deadline role=orient objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

Mina and Erik are planning a weekend at a cabin. Mina is ready to leave, but Erik may have to finish some work first.

::: {.reading translation="Have you decided to come along to the cabin?" dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Har du bestemt deg for å bli med på hytta?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I hope to come along, but the boss says that we have to submit the report on Friday." dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Jeg håper å bli med, men sjefen sier at vi må levere rapporten på fredag.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Can you work on the report tonight?" dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Kan du jobbe med rapporten i kveld?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, I think that I can finish before Thursday." dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Ja, jeg tror at jeg kan bli ferdig før torsdag.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then you should start packing too." dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Da bør du begynne å pakke også.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I promise that I will let you know tomorrow." dialogue_id="cabin" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Jeg lover at jeg skal gi deg beskjed i morgen.
:::

The conversation contains three common ways of placing one verb or idea after another:

- å followed by an infinitive: å bli, å pakke
- at followed by a clause with a subject and a finite verb: at vi må levere
- a bare infinitive after a modal verb: kan jobbe, bør begynne

The choice is not simply a choice between two small words. It tells the listener how the second action or idea is connected to the first verb.

## Three kinds of complement {#three-kinds-complement role=model objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

A verb complement completes the meaning of another verb. In English, compare *hope to come*, *think that I can finish*, and *can work*. Norwegian makes similar distinctions.

## 1. The å-infinitive {#a-infinitive role=model objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

The infinitive (infinitiv) is the basic form of a verb, such as komme, jobbe or spise. The infinitive marker (infinitivsmerket) is å.

::: examples
- no: Erik håper å bli med.
- en: Erik hopes to come along.
- no: Mina planlegger å reise på fredag.
- en: Mina plans to leave on Friday.
- no: De prøver å bli ferdige tidlig.
- en: They are trying to finish early.
:::

In these examples, the person connected to the first verb is understood as the intended or possible performer of the action in the å-infinitive. This identifies the understood subject; it does not claim that the second event actually happens:

- Erik hopes, and Erik is understood as the possible person who will come.
- Mina plans, and Mina is understood as the intended person who will leave.
- They try, and they are understood as the intended people who may finish.

This same-subject pattern is especially common after verbs expressing plans, wishes, attempts, beginnings and endings:

::: examples
- no: Jeg ønsker å lære mer norsk.
- en: I want to learn more Norwegian.
- no: Vi prøver å forstå problemet.
- en: We are trying to understand the problem.
- no: Hun begynner å pakke.
- en: She begins to pack.
- no: Han slutter å jobbe klokka fire.
- en: He stops working at four.
- no: De planlegger å kjøpe bil.
- en: They are planning to buy a car.
:::

A compact pattern is:

**subject + first verb + å + infinitive**

::: examples
- no: Vi prøver å spare penger.
- en: We are trying to save money.
:::

Do not give the infinitive a present-tense ending after å.

::: examples
- no: Hun prøver å komme tidlig.
- en: She is trying to come early.
:::

Not: *Hun prøver å kommer tidlig.*

The first verb carries the tense. The verb after å stays in the infinitive.

## When at introduces a complete clause {#at-complete-clause role=model objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

The conjunction at introduces a subordinate clause (leddsetning). Unlike an å-infinitive, an at-clause normally states its own subject and contains a tense-bearing finite verb. When the finite verb is a modal, a bare infinitive may follow it.

::: examples
- no: Sjefen sier at vi må levere rapporten på fredag.
- en: The boss says that we have to submit the report on Friday.
:::

The at-clause has its own subject, vi, and its finite verb is må.

::: examples
- no: Erik tror at han kan bli ferdig.
- en: Erik thinks that he can finish.
:::

The at-clause has the subject han and the finite modal kan.

::: examples
- no: Mina vet at Erik jobber i kveld.
- en: Mina knows that Erik is working tonight.
:::

Here the at-clause contains Erik and the present-tense verb jobber.

A compact pattern is:

**subject + first verb + at + subject + finite verb**

::: examples
- no: Jeg tror at bussen kommer snart.
- en: I think that the bus is coming soon.
- no: Hun sier at møtet begynner klokka ni.
- en: She says that the meeting begins at nine.
- no: Vi vet at de bor i Bergen.
- en: We know that they live in Bergen.
:::

Verbs of saying, thinking and knowing commonly introduce finite content clauses because what follows is a complete statement. This lesson first models the full form with **at**:

::: examples
- no: Han sier at han er sliten.
- en: He says that he is tired.
- no: Jeg tror at hun har tid.
- en: I think that she has time.
- no: De vet at toget er forsinket.
- en: They know that the train is delayed.
:::

After relevant reporting and thinking verbs, **at** may also be omitted in natural Bokmål. For example, **Jeg tror du forstår** remains a finite content clause with the subject **du** and finite verb **forstår**; it is not a bare infinitive.

The same-subject idea is useful, but it is not the only rule. Norwegian verbs also have their own complementation patterns. In neutral everyday Bokmål, **tro** normally introduces a finite content clause, with or without overt **at**:

::: examples
- no: Jeg tror at jeg forstår.
- en: I think that I understand.
- no: Jeg tror jeg forstår.
- en: I think that I understand.
:::

The form **Jeg tror å forstå** can occur in marked or formal Bokmål, but it is not the neutral everyday pattern practised in this lesson. None of these forms guarantees that understanding is actually achieved.

## Compare å-infinitives and at-clauses {#compare-complements role=contrast objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

Some verbs can occur with either pattern, but the meaning relationship changes.

::: examples
- no: Jeg håper å reise på fredag.
- en: I hope to leave on Friday.
- no: Jeg håper at Mina reiser på fredag.
- en: I hope that Mina leaves on Friday.
:::

In the first sentence, jeg is understood as the person who will leave. In the second, Mina is explicitly the person who will leave.

::: examples
- no: Erik lover å ringe i morgen.
- en: Erik promises to call tomorrow.
- no: Erik lover at han skal ringe i morgen.
- en: Erik promises that he will call tomorrow.
:::

Both sentences can refer to Erik’s future call without guaranteeing that the call happens. The first presents Erik as the intended performer of the promised action. The second presents a complete statement about what is promised. The **at**-clause is useful when the subject or the whole claim needs to be explicit.

Consider another contrast:

::: examples
- no: Vi håper å få svar snart.
- en: We hope to receive an answer soon.
- no: Vi håper at de svarer snart.
- en: We hope that they reply soon.
:::

The first sentence focuses on what vi hope to experience: receiving an answer. The second states what de are hoped to do: reply.

Use the å-infinitive when the first verb naturally takes an infinitive and its subject is understood as the performer of the second action. Use an at-clause when the complement is a complete statement with its own subject and finite verb.

This is a strong working rule, but it must be combined with knowledge of the first verb. Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jeg planlegger å dra tidlig.
- en: I plan to leave early.
- no: Jeg tror at jeg drar tidlig.
- en: I think that I am leaving early.
:::

English allows both *plan to* and *think that*. Norwegian similarly requires the complement pattern associated with the meaning of the first verb.

## Bare infinitives after modal verbs {#bare-infinitives role=model objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

Modal verbs such as kan, skal, vil, må and bør are followed directly by a bare infinitive. A bare infinitive is an infinitive without å.

::: examples
- no: Kan du jobbe i kveld?
- en: Can you work tonight?
- no: Vi må levere rapporten på fredag.
- en: We have to submit the report on Friday.
- no: Jeg skal ringe Mina.
- en: I will call Mina.
- no: Erik bør begynne å pakke.
- en: Erik should start packing.
- no: De vil reise tidlig.
- en: They want to leave early.
:::

The compact pattern is:

**modal verb + bare infinitive**

::: examples
- no: Hun kan komme.
- en: She can come.
:::

Not: *Hun kan å komme.*

Not: *Hun kan kommer.*

After the modal, use the bare infinitive: komme.

A sentence can contain more than one complement pattern. Return to Mina’s advice:

::: examples
- no: Du bør begynne å pakke.
- en: You should start packing.
:::

Here bør is a modal, so it is followed by the bare infinitive begynne. The verb begynne is then followed by an å-infinitive, å pakke.

The structure is:

**bør + begynne + å pakke**

::: examples
- no: Vi skal prøve å dra før åtte.
- en: We will try to leave before eight.
:::

The structure is:

**skal + prøve + å dra**

The bare infinitive rule applies immediately after the modal. It does not remove å from a later verb that requires an å-infinitive.

## Different written forms for different structures {#written-forms role=contrast objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

The written choice among å, at and no marker depends on grammatical structure.

::: examples
- no: Jeg prøver å forstå.
- en: I am trying to understand.
:::

After prøver, the second action is an infinitive performed by the same person, so write å forstå.

::: examples
- no: Jeg tror at du forstår.
- en: I think that you understand.
:::

After **tror**, what follows is a complete statement with the subject **du** and the finite verb **forstår**. This lesson practises the full overt form with **at**; natural Bokmål also permits **Jeg tror du forstår**.

Choose the written form by examining the structure to the right:

- If you need an infinitive, consider å.
- If you have a subject followed by a finite verb, consider the full form with **at**. After relevant reporting and thinking verbs, an omitted **at** may still introduce the same finite content clause.
- If the verb immediately before the infinitive is a modal, use no marker.

Compare all three:

::: examples
- no: Mina håper å komme.
- en: Mina hopes to come.
- no: Erik tror at Mina kommer.
- en: Erik thinks that Mina is coming.
- no: Mina kan komme.
- en: Mina can come.
:::

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes role=contrast objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

**Adding å after a modal**

Incorrect: *Jeg må å jobbe.*

Correct:

::: examples
- no: Jeg må jobbe.
- en: I have to work.
:::

Modal verbs take a bare infinitive.

**Using a present-tense form after å**

Incorrect: *Vi prøver å kommer tidlig.*

Correct:

::: examples
- no: Vi prøver å komme tidlig.
- en: We are trying to come early.
:::

The verb after å stays in the infinitive.

**Omitting the subject from an at-clause**

Incorrect: *Mina sier at kommer på fredag.*

Correct:

::: examples
- no: Mina sier at hun kommer på fredag.
- en: Mina says that she is coming on Friday.
:::

An at-clause normally needs its own subject.

**Neutral everyday complements after tro**

Marked or formal Bokmål can use **tro + å-infinitive**, but the neutral everyday pattern practised here is a finite content clause:

::: examples
- no: Jeg tror at jeg rekker bussen.
- en: I think that I will catch the bus.
- no: Jeg tror jeg rekker bussen.
- en: I think that I will catch the bus.
:::

The second version omits **at** but keeps the subject **jeg** and finite verb **rekker**. This subsection is a register and structure contrast, not a categorical claim that every **tro + å-infinitive** is ungrammatical.

The forms now work together naturally:

- sier at introduces a complete statement
- kan bli uses a bare infinitive after a modal
- skal prøve uses a bare infinitive after a modal
- prøve å gå uses an å-infinitive
- håper å kjøre presents Mina’s own hoped-for action
- tror at introduces Erik’s complete thought

## Quick review {#quick-review role=recap objectives=obj-verb-complementation-01}

Use these questions when choosing a complement:

1. Does the first verb take an action infinitive, often performed by the same person? Use **å + infinitive**.
2. Does the first verb introduce a complete statement with its own subject and finite verb? Use a finite content clause. The full form has **at**; relevant reporting and thinking verbs may allow **at** to be omitted.
3. Is the infinitive immediately after a modal such as kan, skal, vil, må or bør? Use a **bare infinitive**.
