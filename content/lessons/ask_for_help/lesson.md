## At the train station {#at-the-train-station role=orient objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

Mina is travelling from Oslo to Lillehammer. She has bought a ticket on her phone, but she cannot find the correct platform. She asks an employee, Erik, for help.

::: {.reading translation="Excuse me, I can’t find platform four." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Unnskyld, jeg finner ikke spor fire.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Are you going to Lillehammer?" dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Skal du til Lillehammer?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes. The train leaves in ten minutes. Can you help me?" dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ja. Toget går om ti minutter. Kan du hjelpe meg?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, of course. Look at the screen over there." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Ja, selvfølgelig. Se på skjermen der borte.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Okay." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ok.
:::

::: {.reading translation="The train leaves from platform six today. Come with me, and I’ll show you the way." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Toget går fra spor seks i dag. Bli med meg, så viser jeg deg veien.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, thank you." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ja, takk.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Here is platform six." dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Her er spor seks.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Great. Thank you very much for the help!" dialogue_id="station_help" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Flott. Tusen takk for hjelpen!
:::

Mina’s problem is simple but urgent: she cannot find the platform, and her train leaves soon. Her short exchange with Erik follows a useful pattern:

1. Get the other person’s attention.
2. State the obstacle.
3. Ask for help.
4. Listen for the next action.
5. Accept the action.

You can use the same pattern when you cannot find something, cannot understand something, or cannot complete a simple task.

## Getting someone’s attention {#getting-someones-attention role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

Mina begins with **Unnskyld**. This is a natural way to get a stranger’s attention before asking for help.

::: examples
- no: Unnskyld.
- en: Excuse me.
:::

You can also use it before stating your problem.

::: examples
- no: Unnskyld, jeg finner ikke bussen.
- en: Excuse me, I can’t find the bus.
- no: Unnskyld, jeg forstår ikke.
- en: Excuse me, I don’t understand.
- no: Unnskyld, jeg får ikke åpnet døra.
- en: Excuse me, I can’t get the door open.
:::

In these situations, **Unnskyld** does not need to mean that you have done something wrong. It politely signals that you want someone’s attention.

**Checkpoint: identify the situation**

Read the station dialogue again and briefly describe Mina’s obstacle in English. Then point to the Norwegian sentence in which she states it. This activity belongs here because you first need to distinguish the problem from the request for help. A successful response identifies the missing platform as the obstacle and recognizes **jeg finner ikke spor fire** as the sentence that states it.

{{exercise: checkpoint_identify_situation}}

## Stating the obstacle {#stating-the-obstacle role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

A useful way to explain a problem is:

**jeg + verb + ikke**

In Mina’s sentence, **ikke** makes the statement negative.

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner spor fire.
- en: I find platform four.
- no: Jeg finner ikke spor fire.
- en: I can’t find platform four.
:::

The second sentence tells Erik what is preventing Mina from continuing her journey.

**Jeg finner ikke …**

Use **Jeg finner ikke …** when you cannot find a person, place, or object.

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke billetten.
- en: I can’t find the ticket.
- no: Jeg finner ikke hotellet.
- en: I can’t find the hotel.
- no: Jeg finner ikke nøkkelen.
- en: I can’t find the key.
- no: Jeg finner ikke toalettet.
- en: I can’t find the toilet.
:::

Norwegian often uses the present-tense form **finner ikke** where English naturally uses **can’t find**. A word-for-word translation such as *I find not the key* is not natural English, but the Norwegian sentence is normal.

**Jeg forstår ikke …**

Use **Jeg forstår ikke …** when the obstacle is understanding.

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke spørsmålet.
- en: I don’t understand the question.
- no: Jeg forstår ikke beskjeden.
- en: I don’t understand the message.
- no: Jeg forstår ikke dette ordet.
- en: I don’t understand this word.
:::

If the whole situation is unclear, the shorter sentence is enough:

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke.
- en: I don’t understand.
:::

**Jeg får ikke …**

Another common pattern is **Jeg får ikke …**, followed by an action. In practical situations, it can mean that you are trying but cannot make something work.

::: examples
- no: Jeg får ikke åpnet døra.
- en: I can’t get the door open.
- no: Jeg får ikke startet maskinen.
- en: I can’t get the machine started.
- no: Jeg får ikke kjøpt billett.
- en: I can’t manage to buy a ticket.
:::

At this level, learn these as useful complete expressions. The important communicative job is the same: tell the other person what is stopping you.

**Aligned contrasts**

**Obstacle**

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke sporet.
- en: I can’t find the platform.
:::

**Desired result**

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil finne sporet.
- en: I want to find the platform.
:::

**Obstacle**

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke beskjeden.
- en: I don’t understand the message.
:::

**Desired result**

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil forstå beskjeden.
- en: I want to understand the message.
:::

**Obstacle**

::: examples
- no: Jeg får ikke åpnet døra.
- en: I can’t get the door open.
:::

**Desired result**

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil åpne døra.
- en: I want to open the door.
:::

When asking for help, the obstacle is usually more useful than simply stating what you want. **Jeg finner ikke sporet** tells the listener exactly what has gone wrong.

**Checkpoint: choose a useful obstacle statement**

Imagine that Mina has reached the correct platform, but now she cannot find her ticket. Produce one short Norwegian sentence that states this obstacle. This controlled activity comes before the full request so that you can practise naming the problem clearly. A successful response uses **Jeg finner ikke …** with the ticket as the missing object.

## Asking directly for help {#asking-directly-for-help role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

After Mina states the problem, she asks:

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

This is the central request in the chapter. It is polite, direct, and useful in many situations.

- **kan** means can
- **du** means you
- **hjelpe** means help
- **meg** means me

In a statement, **du** normally comes before the verb:

::: examples
- no: Du kan hjelpe meg.
- en: You can help me.
:::

In the question, **kan** comes first:

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

Keep the complete question ready as one practical expression.

You can state the obstacle first and then ask for help:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke hotellet. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I can’t find the hotel. Can you help me?
- no: Jeg forstår ikke beskjeden. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I don’t understand the message. Can you help me?
- no: Jeg får ikke kjøpt billett. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I can’t manage to buy a ticket. Can you help me?
:::

For a polite approach to a stranger, add **Unnskyld**:

::: examples
- no: Unnskyld, jeg finner ikke bussholdeplassen. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Excuse me, I can’t find the bus stop. Can you help me?
:::

This three-part sequence is especially useful:

**Unnskyld + obstacle + Kan du hjelpe meg?**

It gives the listener enough context to respond immediately.

**Common mistakes**

Do not omit **meg** when you mean help me.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Kan du hjelpe?
- en: Can you help?
:::

This can be understood as Can you help?, but it does not specify whom you want the person to help.

More useful here:

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

Do not use statement order when you intend to ask the direct question.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Du kan hjelpe meg?
- en: You can help me?
:::

In casual conversation, intonation can sometimes turn this into a question, but the clear beginner pattern is:

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

Place **ikke** after the verb in these simple obstacle statements.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg ikke finner billetten.
- en: I do not find the ticket.
:::

Correct:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke billetten.
- en: I can’t find the ticket.
:::

**Checkpoint: build the complete approach**

Mina is now at a ticket machine. The screen shows a message that she does not understand. Address a nearby employee in Norwegian. State the obstacle and ask for help.

This activity belongs here because it combines the first three stages of the exchange: getting attention, explaining the problem, and making the request. A successful response uses **Unnskyld**, a natural statement with **Jeg forstår ikke …**, and **Kan du hjelpe meg?**

{{exercise: checkpoint_build_approach}}

## Understanding a helpful response {#understanding-a-helpful-response role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

A helper will often first accept or refuse the request. Erik accepts with:

::: examples
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig.
- en: Yes, of course.
:::

Other short positive responses include:

::: examples
- no: Ja, det kan jeg.
- en: Yes, I can.
- no: Ja, vent litt.
- en: Yes, wait a moment.
- no: Klart det.
- en: Of course.
:::

At A1, you do not need to understand every word in a long reply. Listen first for whether the person is willing to help and then for the action they want you to take.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig.
- en: Yes, of course.
- no: Beklager, jeg kan ikke hjelpe deg.
- en: Sorry, I can’t help you.
:::

In the first response, help is available. In the second, it is not.

A person who agrees to help may immediately give a short next action:

::: examples
- no: Vent litt.
- en: Wait a moment.
- no: Se på skjermen.
- en: Look at the screen.
- no: Prøv igjen.
- en: Try again.
- no: Bli med meg.
- en: Come with me.
- no: Gå til informasjonsskranken.
- en: Go to the information desk.
- no: Trykk her.
- en: Press here.
:::

Treat these as practical instructions to recognize. You are not learning to give a full set of instructions yourself; your job is to understand enough of the response to continue.

**Response plus next action**

Often the reply has two parts: acceptance and action.

::: examples
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig. Vent litt.
- en: Yes, of course. Wait a moment.
- no: Ja, det kan jeg. Trykk her.
- en: Yes, I can. Press here.
- no: Klart det. Bli med meg.
- en: Of course. Come with me.
- no: Ja. Se på skjermen der borte.
- en: Yes. Look at the screen over there.
:::

In the station dialogue, Erik gives Mina two pieces of useful information. First, the train leaves from platform six. Then he says:

::: examples
- no: Bli med meg, så viser jeg deg veien.
- en: Come with me, and I’ll show you the way.
:::

Mina does not need to produce this sentence herself. She needs to recognize **Bli med meg** as the action Erik wants her to take.

**Checkpoint: listen for the next action**

Read each response below as a complete message and state, in English, what the learner should do next.

::: examples
- no: Ja, vent litt.
- en: Yes, wait a moment.
- no: Klart det. Trykk her.
- en: Of course. Press here.
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig. Bli med meg.
- en: Yes, of course. Come with me.
:::

This recognition activity belongs here because asking successfully is not enough: you must also act on the answer. A successful response distinguishes waiting, pressing the indicated place, and following the helper.

{{exercise: checkpoint_listen_next_action}}

## Accepting the next action {#accepting-the-next-action role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

Once you understand the instruction, show that you accept it. Mina first says **Ok**, then later **Ja, takk**.

::: examples
- no: Ok.
- en: Okay.
- no: Ja, takk.
- en: Yes, thank you.
- no: Greit, takk.
- en: All right, thank you.
:::

These short replies keep the exchange moving. They tell the helper that you have understood and agree to the next step.

Compare the following exchanges:

**At a machine**

::: examples
- no: Jeg får ikke kjøpt billett. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I can’t manage to buy a ticket. Can you help me?
- no: Ja. Trykk her.
- en: Yes. Press here.
- no: Ok, takk.
- en: Okay, thank you.
:::

**In a building**

::: examples
- no: Unnskyld, jeg finner ikke resepsjonen. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Excuse me, I can’t find reception. Can you help me?
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig. Bli med meg.
- en: Yes, of course. Come with me.
- no: Ja, takk.
- en: Yes, thank you.
:::

**In a classroom**

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke dette ordet. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I don’t understand this word. Can you help me?
- no: Ja. Se på eksemplet.
- en: Yes. Look at the example.
- no: Greit, takk.
- en: All right, thank you.
:::

The acceptance should fit the response. If someone says **Vent litt**, **Ok** is natural. If someone offers to lead you somewhere, **Ja, takk** naturally accepts the offer.

**Checkpoint: complete the communicative sequence**

Imagine that you cannot open a door at the station. An employee replies:

::: examples
- no: Ja, selvfølgelig. Prøv igjen nå.
- en: Yes, of course. Try again now.
:::

State your obstacle and request before this reply, then add a short Norwegian acceptance after it. This activity belongs here because it practises the complete sequence rather than one isolated phrase. A successful response clearly states the door problem, asks **Kan du hjelpe meg?**, understands that the employee wants another attempt, and accepts with a suitable short expression.

{{exercise: checkpoint_complete_sequence}}

## Returning to Mina and Erik {#returning-to-mina-and-erik role=contrast objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

A few minutes later, Mina is standing by platform six. She looks at the departure screen but does not understand a message beside the train number.

::: {.reading translation="Excuse me, Erik. I don’t understand this message. Can you help me?" dialogue_id="station_help_followup" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Unnskyld, Erik. Jeg forstår ikke denne beskjeden. Kan du hjelpe meg?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes. It says that the train is five minutes late." dialogue_id="station_help_followup" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Ja. Det står at toget er fem minutter forsinket.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Oh, okay." dialogue_id="station_help_followup" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Å, ok.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Wait here. The train will arrive soon." dialogue_id="station_help_followup" speaker_id="erik" speaker_name="Erik" character_id="erik"}
Vent her. Toget kommer snart.
:::

::: {.reading translation="All right, thank you." dialogue_id="station_help_followup" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Greit, takk.
:::

Mina uses the same pattern in a new situation. Her first obstacle involved finding a place:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke spor fire.
- en: I can’t find platform four.
:::

Her second obstacle involves understanding information:

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke denne beskjeden.
- en: I don’t understand this message.
:::

The request stays the same:

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

She then listens for the action:

::: examples
- no: Vent her.
- en: Wait here.
:::

Finally, she accepts it:

::: examples
- no: Greit, takk.
- en: All right, thank you.
:::

This repetition is useful because real requests for help vary in their details, but the communicative sequence remains stable.

## Practising in new situations {#practising-in-new-situations role=model objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

**Practice 1: obstacle or request?**

Return to Mina’s two exchanges. Separate the sentences that describe her obstacles from the sentence she uses to request help. Explain briefly what job each kind of sentence does.

This practice belongs here to strengthen the distinction between giving context and making a request. A successful response recognizes **Jeg finner ikke …** and **Jeg forstår ikke …** as obstacle statements, while **Kan du hjelpe meg?** is the request.

{{exercise: practice_1_obstacle_or_request}}

**Practice 2: state different obstacles**

Create short Norwegian obstacle statements for these contexts: a missing key, an unclear question, and a ticket machine that you cannot get started. Use the familiar patterns from the chapter rather than adding extra information.

This practice moves the same communicative skill beyond the station dialogue while keeping the language controlled. A successful response chooses a suitable pattern—**Jeg finner ikke …**, **Jeg forstår ikke …**, or **Jeg får ikke …**—for each type of obstacle.

{{exercise: practice_2_state_different_obstacles}}

**Practice 3: add the request**

Take each obstacle from the previous activity and turn it into a brief request for help. Include a polite attention-getter where you are speaking to a stranger.

This step belongs after obstacle practice because the listener needs both context and a clear request. A successful response links the obstacle naturally to **Kan du hjelpe meg?** and uses **Unnskyld** appropriately.

{{exercise: practice_3_add_request}}

**Practice 4: understand and accept**

Imagine that a helper responds to your three requests with one of these actions: wait a moment, look at the screen, or come with the helper. For each situation, describe the action in English and give a short Norwegian acceptance.

This practice checks whether you can move from asking to understanding and continuing. A successful response correctly interprets the action and answers naturally with **Ok**, **Ja, takk**, or **Greit, takk**.

{{exercise: practice_4_understand_and_accept}}

**Practice 5: follow the whole exchange**

Create a short exchange at a station information desk. You cannot understand a message about your train. Get the employee’s attention, state the obstacle, and ask for help. Give the employee a brief positive response and one simple next action. End with your acceptance.

This controlled exchange belongs near the end because it brings all parts together in a familiar context. A successful response demonstrates the complete sequence: obstacle, request, understood response, and acceptance. Keep the exchange short and practical.

{{exercise: practice_5_follow_whole_exchange}}

## Common communication problems {#common-communication-problems role=contrast objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

**Giving only the problem**

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke billetten.
- en: I can’t find the ticket.
:::

This may be enough if the context is obvious, but the listener may not know what you want. Add the request:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke billetten. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I can’t find the ticket. Can you help me?
:::

**Asking without context**

::: examples
- no: Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: Can you help me?
:::

This is correct, but the helper will probably need to ask what the problem is. A short obstacle statement makes the request more efficient:

::: examples
- no: Jeg forstår ikke denne beskjeden. Kan du hjelpe meg?
- en: I don’t understand this message. Can you help me?
:::

**Ignoring the next action**

If the helper says **Vent litt**, repeating **Kan du hjelpe meg?** does not show that you understood. Accept the instruction:

::: examples
- no: Ok, takk.
- en: Okay, thank you.
:::

**Trying to understand every word**

A reply may contain unfamiliar language. Listen for familiar action expressions such as **vent litt**, **se på skjermen**, **prøv igjen**, and **bli med meg**. Recognizing the next action can be enough to continue successfully.

## Retrieval and review {#retrieval-and-review role=recap objectives=obj-ask-for-help-01}

Without looking back, produce one short exchange in which you cannot find something. Get attention, state the obstacle, ask for help, include a positive response with one next action, and accept that action. A successful exchange demonstrates the full help-seeking sequence clearly and naturally.
