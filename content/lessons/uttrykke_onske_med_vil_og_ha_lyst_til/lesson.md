## A Saturday in Bergen {#a-saturday-in-bergen role=orient objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Emma and Jonas are staying with their friend Nora in Bergen. On Saturday morning, they are deciding what to do.

::: {.reading translation="What do you want to do today?" dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Hva vil dere gjøre i dag?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I want to see Bryggen first. I’ve never been there." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=emma speaker_name="Emma" voice_profile="feminine"}
Jeg vil se Bryggen først. Jeg har aldri vært der.
:::

::: {.reading translation="All right. Afterwards I feel like walking up Mount Fløyen." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Greit. Etterpå har jeg lyst til å gå opp Fløyen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Walk up? I want to take the railway." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=emma speaker_name="Emma" voice_profile="feminine"}
Gå opp? Jeg vil ta banen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="We can decide that later. Do you feel like having breakfast first?" dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Vi kan bestemme det senere. Har dere lyst på frokost først?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, I feel like having coffee and something warm to eat." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, jeg har lyst på kaffe og noe varmt å spise.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I only want tea. And then I want to leave straight away." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=emma speaker_name="Emma" voice_profile="feminine"}
Jeg vil bare ha te. Og så vil jeg dra med en gang.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then we’ll go to the café on the corner." dialogue_id=bergen-saturday-morning speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Da går vi til kafeen på hjørnet.
:::

The three friends are all expressing wants, but they do not always use the same expression. Emma often states a clear choice or intention with **vil**: she wants to see Bryggen, take the railway, and leave soon. Jonas describes what appeals to him at the moment with **har lyst til å** or **har lyst på**: he feels like walking, and coffee sounds good to him.

That contrast is the focus of this chapter.

## A direct want or intention: *vil* {#direct-want-or-intention-vil role=model objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Use **vil** when you state a direct desire, choice, or intention. It is followed by the infinitive without **å**.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil se Bryggen.
- en: I want to see Bryggen.
- no: Emma vil ta banen.
- en: Emma wants to take the railway.
- no: Vi vil dra tidlig.
- en: We want to leave early.
- no: Vil dere spise nå?
- en: Do you want to eat now?
:::

The structure is:

::: {.rule}
**vil + infinitive**
:::

Notice that Norwegian does not use **å** after **vil**.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil gå hjem.
- en: I want to go home.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg vil å gå hjem.
- en: I want to go home.
:::

This form is incorrect because **vil** takes the infinitive directly.

In the opening dialogue, Emma has a definite plan:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil se Bryggen først.
- en: I want to see Bryggen first.
:::

She is not merely saying that sightseeing sounds pleasant. She is putting forward a clear intention for the day.

Compare some other direct choices:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil bestille fiskesuppe.
- en: I want to order fish soup.
- no: Han vil bo i Oslo.
- en: He wants to live in Oslo.
- no: De vil reise i juni.
- en: They want to travel in June.
- no: Vi vil ikke vente lenger.
- en: We don’t want to wait any longer.
:::

With a thing rather than an activity, **vil ha** is common:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil ha te.
- en: I want tea.
- no: Hun vil ha en ny sykkel.
- en: She wants a new bicycle.
- no: Barna vil ha is.
- en: The children want ice cream.
:::

Here **ha** means *have*, so the literal structure is “want to have.” In ordinary English, the natural translation is often simply *want*.

## Checkpoint: identify clear decisions {#checkpoint-identify-clear-decisions role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Read several short statements about Emma, Jonas, and Nora’s morning. Decide which statements present a clear choice or intended action. Reformulate those ideas with **vil + infinitive**, or with **vil ha** when the desired item is a thing.

This activity belongs here because it checks the basic frame before introducing the contrasting expression. Use the Bergen outing as the context: transport, breakfast, sightseeing, and departure time. A successful response will use **vil** for definite wants and will omit **å** after **vil**.

## What appeals to you: *ha lyst til å* {#what-appeals-to-you-ha-lyst-til-a role=model objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Use **ha lyst til å** when an activity appeals to someone or when someone feels inclined to do it. English often uses *feel like*.

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst til å gå opp Fløyen.
- en: I feel like walking up Mount Fløyen.
- no: Nora har lyst til å spise ute.
- en: Nora feels like eating out.
- no: Vi har lyst til å besøke museet.
- en: We feel like visiting the museum.
- no: Har du lyst til å bli med?
- en: Do you feel like coming along?
:::

The structure is:

::: {.rule}
**ha lyst til å + infinitive**
:::

Unlike **vil**, this expression requires **å** before the following verb.

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst til å bade.
- en: I feel like swimming.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg har lyst til bade.
- en: I feel like swimming.
:::

This form is incorrect because an activity after **lyst til** needs **å**.

The form of **ha** changes in the usual way. In the present tense, it is **har**:

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst til å lage middag.
- en: I feel like making dinner.
- no: Hun har ikke lyst til å gå ut.
- en: She doesn’t feel like going out.
:::

The word **lyst** does not change according to the person:

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst til å danse.
- en: I feel like dancing.
- no: De har lyst til å danse.
- en: They feel like dancing.
:::

In the dialogue, Jonas says:

::: examples
- no: Etterpå har jeg lyst til å gå opp Fløyen.
- en: Afterwards I feel like walking up Mount Fløyen.
:::

Walking appeals to him. His sentence sounds less like a fixed plan than Emma’s:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil ta banen.
- en: I want to take the railway.
:::

The difference is not that one wish is strong and the other weak in every situation. The important choice is how the speaker presents the wish:

- **vil** presents a direct want or intention;
- **ha lyst til å** presents an activity as something the person feels inclined to do.

## Aligned contrasts {#aligned-contrasts role=contrast objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

The same activity can be expressed in two ways, depending on the intended meaning.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil gå hjem nå.
- en: I want to go home now.
- no: Jeg har lyst til å gå en tur.
- en: I feel like going for a walk.
:::

The first sentence states a direct wish or decision. The second describes an appealing activity.

::: examples
- no: Sara vil lage middag i kveld.
- en: Sara wants to make dinner tonight.
- no: Sara har lyst til å lage noe nytt.
- en: Sara feels like making something new.
:::

In the first sentence, making dinner is Sara’s intended action. In the second, experimenting with something new appeals to her.

::: examples
- no: Vi vil besøke familien i helgen.
- en: We want to visit the family this weekend.
- no: Vi har lyst til å besøke den nye utstillingen.
- en: We feel like visiting the new exhibition.
:::

Again, **vil** points toward a chosen plan. **Har lyst til å** presents an attractive possibility.

A negative contrast is also useful:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil ikke kjøre i kveld.
- en: I don’t want to drive tonight.
- no: Jeg har ikke lyst til å kjøre i regnet.
- en: I don’t feel like driving in the rain.
:::

The first speaker directly rejects driving. The second describes a lack of inclination, connected here to the weather.

## Checkpoint: interpret the speaker’s perspective {#checkpoint-interpret-speakers-perspective role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Consider pairs of statements about the same weekend activity. Explain which statement presents a direct choice and which presents something that currently appeals to the speaker. Then translate that distinction into English using *want to* and *feel like* where natural.

This checkpoint belongs after the aligned contrasts because correct form alone is not enough: the learner must notice the speaker’s perspective. Use familiar activities such as cooking, travelling, walking, visiting friends, and staying home. A successful response will connect **vil** with direct desire or intention and **ha lyst til å** with inclination toward an activity.

## Things that appeal to you: *ha lyst på* {#things-that-appeal-to-you-ha-lyst-pa role=model objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

When the desired object is a thing, food, or drink, use **ha lyst på + noun**. Do not add **å**.

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst på kaffe.
- en: I feel like having coffee.
- no: Hun har lyst på is.
- en: She feels like having ice cream.
- no: Barna har lyst på pizza.
- en: The children feel like having pizza.
- no: Har dere lyst på frokost?
- en: Do you feel like having breakfast?
:::

The structure is:

::: {.rule}
**ha lyst på + noun**
:::

Compare the two **lyst** frames:

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst til å spise.
- en: I feel like eating.
- no: Jeg har lyst på en sandwich.
- en: I feel like having a sandwich.
:::

Use **til å** before an activity and **på** before a thing.

::: examples
- no: Vi har lyst til å lage suppe.
- en: We feel like making soup.
- no: Vi har lyst på suppe.
- en: We feel like having soup.
:::

This is the distinction Nora and Jonas use at the café:

::: examples
- no: Har dere lyst på frokost først?
- en: Do you feel like having breakfast first?
- no: Jeg har lyst på kaffe og noe varmt å spise.
- en: I feel like having coffee and something warm to eat.
:::

Jonas is describing what sounds appealing to him at that moment.

Now compare **vil ha** and **ha lyst på**:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil ha en kaffe.
- en: I want a coffee.
- no: Jeg har lyst på kaffe.
- en: I feel like having coffee.
:::

Both sentences can be natural. The first is a direct statement of what the speaker wants. The second emphasizes appetite, desire, or present inclination.

Another pair:

::: examples
- no: Mina vil ha den blå jakka.
- en: Mina wants the blue jacket.
- no: Mina har lyst på en ny jakke.
- en: Mina feels like getting a new jacket.
:::

The first points to a direct choice: the blue jacket. The second describes the general appeal of getting a new jacket.

## Checkpoint: choose the correct *lyst* frame {#checkpoint-choose-correct-lyst-frame role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Complete short contextual statements using **har lyst til å** for activities and **har lyst på** for things. Keep the situations connected to the café and the day in Bergen: ordering a drink, eating something warm, visiting a place, taking a walk, or buying a small souvenir.

This activity belongs here because learners often understand the meaning of **lyst** but confuse **til å** and **på**. A successful response will place **til å** before an infinitive and **på** before a noun, without mixing the two patterns.

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes role=contrast objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

**Adding *å* after *vil***

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil besøke museet.
- en: I want to visit the museum.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg vil å besøke museet.
- en: I want to visit the museum.
:::

Use the infinitive directly after **vil**.

**Omitting *å* after *lyst til***

::: examples
- no: Hun har lyst til å reise.
- en: She feels like travelling.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Hun har lyst til reise.
- en: She feels like travelling.
:::

An activity requires **til å + infinitive**.

**Using *på* before an activity**

::: examples
- no: De har lyst til å svømme.
- en: They feel like swimming.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ De har lyst på å svømme.
- en: They feel like swimming.
:::

Use **på** before a noun, not before an infinitive activity.

**Using *til å* before a thing**

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst på sjokolade.
- en: I feel like having chocolate.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg har lyst til å sjokolade.
- en: I feel like having chocolate.
:::

**Sjokolade** is a thing, so use **på**.

**Treating *vil* and *har lyst til å* as mechanically interchangeable**

::: examples
- no: Oskar vil flytte til Trondheim.
- en: Oskar wants to move to Trondheim.
:::

This presents moving as his direct desire or intention.

::: examples
- no: Oskar har lyst til å besøke Trondheim.
- en: Oskar feels like visiting Trondheim.
:::

This presents a visit as something appealing. Replacing one form with the other changes how the wish is framed, even when both sentences remain grammatical.

## Back to Bergen {#back-to-bergen role=model objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

After breakfast, the weather changes. The friends reconsider their plan.

::: {.reading translation="It’s raining quite a lot now. Do you still want to go to Fløyen?" dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Nå regner det ganske mye. Vil dere fortsatt dra til Fløyen?
:::

::: {.reading translation="No, I don’t feel like walking up in this weather." dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Nei, jeg har ikke lyst til å gå opp i dette været.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I still want to take the railway, but I also feel like seeing a museum." dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=emma speaker_name="Emma" voice_profile="feminine"}
Jeg vil fortsatt ta banen, men jeg har også lyst til å se et museum.
:::

::: {.reading translation="What do you feel most like doing?" dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Hva har du mest lyst til?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I think I feel most like going to a museum." dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=emma speaker_name="Emma" voice_profile="feminine"}
Jeg tror jeg har mest lyst til å gå på museum.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Agreed. And afterwards I feel like having something sweet." dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Enig. Og etterpå har jeg lyst på noe søtt.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then I want to show you a café I like." dialogue_id=bergen-changing-plans speaker_id=nora speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Da vil jeg vise dere en kafé jeg liker.
:::

The new dialogue reuses all three patterns:

::: examples
- no: Vil dere fortsatt dra til Fløyen?
- en: Do you still want to go to Fløyen?
:::

This asks whether the original intention remains.

::: examples
- no: Jeg har ikke lyst til å gå opp i dette været.
- en: I don’t feel like walking up in this weather.
:::

Jonas no longer feels inclined to do the activity.

::: examples
- no: Jeg har lyst på noe søtt.
- en: I feel like having something sweet.
:::

The desired item is a thing, so Jonas uses **på**.

Emma uses both expressions in one turn:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil fortsatt ta banen, men jeg har også lyst til å se et museum.
- en: I still want to take the railway, but I also feel like seeing a museum.
:::

Her first idea remains a direct want. Her second idea is another activity that now appeals to her. This shows why the two expressions are useful together: they let a speaker distinguish a chosen intention from a present inclination.

## Guided practice: changing plans {#guided-practice-changing-plans role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Imagine that the rain forces Emma, Jonas, and Nora to revise the rest of their day. Write short responses for them using the information in the context. Express definite decisions with **vil**, appealing activities with **har lyst til å**, and appealing food or objects with **har lyst på**.

This practice belongs here because the learner must now choose among all three frames in a changing situation rather than complete isolated sentences. A successful response will match each structure to the intended meaning and preserve the correct grammar after **vil**, **til å**, and **på**.

{{exercise: changing_plans}}

## Personalised but bounded practice {#personalised-but-bounded-practice role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Write a short plan for an ordinary free afternoon. Include one direct intention, one activity you feel inclined to do, and one food, drink, or thing that appeals to you. Keep each statement specific enough for the contrast to be clear.

For example:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vil gjøre ferdig leksene først.
- en: I want to finish my homework first.
- no: Etterpå har jeg lyst til å møte en venn.
- en: Afterwards I feel like meeting a friend.
- no: I kveld har jeg lyst på pizza.
- en: This evening I feel like having pizza.
:::

This activity comes late in the chapter because it requires independent choice while still keeping the task tightly focused. A successful response will demonstrate the same distinction in a new situation: direct desire or intention with **vil**, inclination toward an activity with **ha lyst til å**, and inclination toward a thing with **ha lyst på**.

{{exercise: personalised_bounded_practice}}

## Retrieval and review {#retrieval-and-review role=recap objectives=obj-uttrykke-onske-med-vil-og-ha-lyst-til-01}

Without looking back, recall the three frames and produce three connected sentences about tomorrow: one with **vil + infinitive** or **vil ha**, one with **har lyst til å + infinitive**, and one with **har lyst på + noun**. Then briefly explain in English why each form fits its sentence.
