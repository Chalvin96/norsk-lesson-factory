## A plan for Saturday {#saturday-plan role=orient objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Mina and Jonas are deciding what to do on Saturday. They want to spend time together, but the weather may affect their plans.

::: {.reading translation="What do you want to do on Saturday?" dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Hva vil du gjøre på lørdag?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I want to go for a walk and eat lunch out." dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Jeg vil gå en tur og spise lunsj ute.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That sounds nice, but it is going to rain." dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Det høres hyggelig ut, men det skal regne.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Shall we go to a café or cook at home?" dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Skal vi gå på kafé eller lage mat hjemme?
:::

::: {.reading translation="We can go to a café, because I feel like having cake." dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Vi kan gå på kafé, for jeg har lyst på kake.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Great! The café opens at ten, so we can meet at half past ten." dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Flott! Kafeen åpner klokka ti, så vi kan møtes halv elleve.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, and afterwards we can go for a short walk." dialogue_id="saturday-plan" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ja, og etterpå kan vi gå en kort tur.
:::

The conversation moves forward through a series of small connections. Jonas combines two activities. Mina introduces a contrast. Jonas presents a choice. Mina gives a reason. Finally, Jonas draws a practical conclusion.

The five joining words doing this work are:

- **og** — *and*
- **men** — *but*
- **eller** — *or*
- **for** — *because, for*
- **så** — *so*

These words are called coordinating conjunctions (**sideordnende konjunksjoner**). They connect words or simple clauses that have a similar role in the sentence. A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb, such as **jeg lager mat** — *I am cooking*.

The important choice is not only which English translation looks familiar. You must decide what relationship you want to express: addition, contrast, choice, reason, or result.

## Adding information with *og* {#adding-with-og role=model objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Use **og** when both connected ideas belong together. It often corresponds directly to English *and*.

It can connect individual words:

::: examples
- no: Mina bestiller kaffe og kake.
- en: Mina orders coffee and cake.
- no: Kafeen er liten og koselig.
- en: The café is small and cosy.
:::

It can also connect actions:

::: examples
- no: Jonas går en tur og spiser lunsj ute.
- en: Jonas goes for a walk and eats lunch out.
:::

Here Jonas performs both actions. Because the subject is the same, Norwegian does not need to repeat **Jonas** before the second verb.

You can also connect two complete clauses:

::: examples
- no: Mina bestiller kaffe, og Jonas bestiller te.
- en: Mina orders coffee, and Jonas orders tea.
:::

Each part has its own subject and verb:

::: examples
- no: Mina bestiller kaffe.
- en: Mina orders coffee.
- no: Jonas bestiller te.
- en: Jonas orders tea.
:::

::: {.rule}
The compact rule is: use **og** when you want to add one word, action, or simple idea to another.
:::

A common mistake is to treat **og** as if it means “and then” in every sentence. It can show that two things both happen, but it does not automatically emphasize a result.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjøper brød og lager lunsj.
- en: I buy bread and make lunch.
- no: Jeg har ikke brød, så jeg går til butikken.
- en: I do not have bread, so I go to the shop.
:::

The first sentence adds two actions. The second says that one fact leads to another.

**Request: Notice Addition**

**Purpose:** Help the learner recognize when **og** expresses simple addition.

**Context:** Short descriptions of Mina and Jonas preparing for their café visit.

**Learner action:** Identify which words or actions belong together and should be connected with **og**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows that the learner can recognize addition without confusing it with choice, contrast, reason, or result.

{{exercise: notice_addition}}

**Request: Build with *og***

**Purpose:** Move from recognition to controlled production.

**Context:** Everyday pairs such as food and drink, two weekend activities, or two qualities of a place.

**Learner action:** Combine each pair into one natural Norwegian sentence using **og**, avoiding unnecessary repetition when the subject is shared.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates correct use of **og** to connect words or actions with equal roles.

{{exercise: build_with_og}}

## Showing contrast with *men* {#showing-contrast-with-men role=contrast objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Use **men** when the second idea contrasts with the first. English normally uses *but*.

In the dialogue, Mina agrees that Jonas’s idea sounds pleasant, but she introduces a problem:

::: examples
- no: Det høres hyggelig ut, men det skal regne.
- en: That sounds nice, but it is going to rain.
:::

The rain does not cancel the pleasant idea completely. It changes how Mina and Jonas must think about it.

More examples:

::: examples
- no: Kafeen er liten, men den er populær.
- en: The café is small, but it is popular.
- no: Jeg liker kaffe, men jeg drikker te i dag.
- en: I like coffee, but I am drinking tea today.
- no: Jonas vil gå en lang tur, men Mina vil gå en kort tur.
- en: Jonas wants to go for a long walk, but Mina wants to go for a short walk.
:::

You can also connect shorter elements:

::: examples
- no: Kaken er god, men dyr.
- en: The cake is good but expensive.
:::

::: {.rule}
The compact rule is: use **men** when the second word or idea is different from what the first one might lead you to expect.
:::

Compare addition and contrast:

::: examples
- no: Kafeen er liten og koselig.
- en: The café is small and cosy.
- no: Kafeen er liten, men koselig.
- en: The café is small but cosy.
:::

In the first sentence, *small* and *cosy* are simply two descriptions. In the second, the speaker suggests that being small could be a disadvantage, yet the café is cosy.

A common mistake is to use **og** merely because both ideas are true. The relationship between them matters more than the fact that both appear in the same sentence.

::: examples
- no: Jeg er trøtt, men jeg går på jobb.
- en: I am tired, but I am going to work.
:::

Both statements are true, but the second contrasts with the expectation created by the first.

**Request: Hear the Contrast**

**Purpose:** Make the meaning of **men** clear before the learner produces it.

**Context:** Simple situations involving weather, preferences, prices, and weekend plans.

**Learner action:** Decide whether the second idea is merely additional or whether it creates a contrast, then mark the connections that require **men**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner chooses **men** because of meaning, not simply because two clauses are present.

{{exercise: hear_the_contrast}}

**Request: Complete a Change of Plan**

**Purpose:** Practise producing a contrast in a familiar context.

**Context:** Mina and Jonas encounter small problems with their Saturday plan, such as rain, a busy café, or different preferences.

**Learner action:** Complete short two-part statements with a contrasting idea joined by **men**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows that the learner can use **men** to connect an expectation with a different reality.

{{exercise: complete_a_change_of_plan}}

## Presenting alternatives with *eller* {#presenting-alternatives-with-eller role=model objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Use **eller** to connect alternatives. It corresponds to English *or*.

In the dialogue, Jonas asks:

::: examples
- no: Skal vi gå på kafé eller lage mat hjemme?
- en: Shall we go to a café or cook at home?
:::

The two alternatives are **gå på kafé** and **lage mat hjemme**. Mina and Jonas are deciding between them.

**Eller** can connect nouns:

::: examples
- no: Vil du ha kaffe eller te?
- en: Would you like coffee or tea?
:::

It can connect descriptions:

::: examples
- no: Er kafeen åpen eller stengt?
- en: Is the café open or closed?
:::

It can connect complete clauses:

::: examples
- no: Vi kan møtes klokka ti, eller vi kan møtes klokka elleve.
- en: We can meet at ten, or we can meet at eleven.
:::

It is especially common in questions, but it also appears in statements:

::: examples
- no: På lørdag spiser vi på kafé eller hjemme.
- en: On Saturday we will eat at a café or at home.
:::

::: {.rule}
The compact rule is: use **eller** when the connected words or ideas are alternatives.
:::

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Mina kjøper kaffe og kake.
- en: Mina buys coffee and cake.
- no: Mina kjøper kaffe eller kake.
- en: Mina buys coffee or cake.
:::

With **og**, she buys both. With **eller**, the sentence presents alternatives.

A common mistake is to translate English *or* without checking whether the Norwegian sentence actually presents a choice. If both things happen, use **og**. If one possibility is set against another, use **eller**.

**Request: Separate Both from Either**

**Purpose:** Establish the key meaning contrast between **og** and **eller**.

**Context:** Café orders and simple weekend activities.

**Learner action:** Determine whether each situation includes both items or presents alternatives, then supply the appropriate joining word.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates accurate distinction between addition and choice.

{{exercise: separate_both_from_either}}

**Request: Ask about Alternatives**

**Purpose:** Practise using **eller** in useful everyday questions.

**Context:** Mina asks Jonas about meeting times, drinks, food, and indoor or outdoor activities.

**Learner action:** Form short questions that connect two alternatives with **eller**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows that the learner can place **eller** naturally between words or actions representing a real choice.

{{exercise: ask_about_alternatives}}

## Giving a reason with *for* {#giving-a-reason-with-for role=model objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Use **for** when the second clause gives a reason for the first. In this use, **for** means *because* or the more formal English *for*.

Mina says:

::: examples
- no: Vi kan gå på kafé, for jeg har lyst på kake.
- en: We can go to a café, because I feel like having cake.
:::

The first clause gives the suggestion. The second clause explains why Mina makes it.

More examples:

::: examples
- no: Jonas tar med en paraply, for det skal regne.
- en: Jonas brings an umbrella because it is going to rain.
- no: Vi går inn, for det er kaldt ute.
- en: We go inside because it is cold outside.
- no: Jeg bestiller te, for jeg liker ikke kaffe.
- en: I order tea because I do not like coffee.
:::

::: {.rule}
The compact rule is: state the decision or situation first, then use **for** to add its reason.
:::

A useful way to check the meaning is to ask “Why?”

::: examples
- no: Hvorfor tar Jonas med en paraply?
- en: Why does Jonas bring an umbrella?
- no: Han tar med en paraply, for det skal regne.
- en: He brings an umbrella because it is going to rain.
:::

Do not confuse this conjunction with **for** in expressions such as **for Mina** — *for Mina* or **for dyr** — *too expensive*. In this chapter, the target use of **for** connects a statement with its reason.

Also avoid using **for** when the second clause is a consequence rather than an explanation.

::: examples
- no: Det regner, for himmelen er mørk.
- en: It is raining, because the sky is dark.
:::

This is usually illogical: a dark sky does not normally cause or explain the fact that rain is falling. The speaker may instead mean that the dark sky is evidence, but that is not the simple reason relationship being practised here.

A clearer reason is:

::: examples
- no: Vi tar med paraplyer, for det regner.
- en: We are bringing umbrellas because it is raining.
:::

**Request: Find the Reason**

**Purpose:** Help the learner identify which clause explains the other.

**Context:** Decisions connected with Mina and Jonas’s Saturday outing.

**Learner action:** Match each decision with a sensible reason and connect the two clauses using **for**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner can place the decision first and its reason after **for**.

{{exercise: find_the_reason}}

**Request: Explain an Everyday Decision**

**Purpose:** Develop controlled use of **for** beyond the original dialogue.

**Context:** Simple decisions about clothing, transport, food, and meeting places.

**Learner action:** Add a short reason to a given statement using **for**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows that the learner can produce a logical reason relationship between two simple clauses.

{{exercise: explain_an_everyday_decision}}

## Showing a result with *så* {#showing-a-result-with-sa role=model objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

Use **så** when the second clause is a result or consequence of the first. It corresponds to English *so*.

Jonas says:

::: examples
- no: Kafeen åpner klokka ti, så vi kan møtes halv elleve.
- en: The café opens at ten, so we can meet at half past ten.
:::

The opening time makes the meeting time possible. The first clause gives the situation; the second gives its result.

More examples:

::: examples
- no: Det regner, så vi går på kafé.
- en: It is raining, so we are going to a café.
- no: Mina er sulten, så hun bestiller lunsj.
- en: Mina is hungry, so she orders lunch.
- no: Bussen er forsinket, så Jonas kommer sent.
- en: The bus is delayed, so Jonas arrives late.
:::

::: {.rule}
The compact rule is: state a situation first, then use **så** to introduce what happens because of it.
:::

Compare **for** and **så** carefully:

::: examples
- no: Vi går på kafé, for det regner.
- en: We are going to a café because it is raining.
- no: Det regner, så vi går på kafé.
- en: It is raining, so we are going to a café.
:::

The facts are similar, but the direction is different.

With **for**, the action comes first and the reason follows:

**action + for + reason**

With **så**, the situation comes first and the result follows:

**situation + så + result**

Another aligned contrast:

::: examples
- no: Jeg spiser lunsj, for jeg er sulten.
- en: I am eating lunch because I am hungry.
- no: Jeg er sulten, så jeg spiser lunsj.
- en: I am hungry, so I am eating lunch.
:::

A common mistake is to choose between **for** and **så** only by translating an English sentence word for word. Instead, ask what comes second. If the second clause explains the first, use **for**. If the second clause follows as a result, use **så**.

**Request: Reason or Result**

**Purpose:** Establish the central contrast between **for** and **så**.

**Context:** Paired descriptions of weather, hunger, transport, and plans.

**Learner action:** Examine the order of the two ideas and choose the joining word that makes the second clause either a reason or a result.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner can distinguish explanation from consequence. Clause order is context; the scored goal is correct choice between **for** and **så**.

{{exercise: reason_or_result}}

**Request: Reverse the Connection**

**Purpose:** Strengthen understanding by expressing the same basic facts in two directions.

**Context:** Facts such as rain and choosing an indoor activity, hunger and ordering food, or a delayed bus and arriving late.

**Learner action:** First connect an action to its reason with **for**. Then reverse the order of the ideas and connect the situation to its result with **så**.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows that the learner understands how changing the order changes the required joining word.

{{exercise: reverse_the_connection}}

## Connecting complete clauses {#connecting-complete-clauses role=contrast objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

All five joining words can appear between simple clauses, but the relationship changes.

Compare these sentences:

::: examples
- no: Mina drikker kaffe, og Jonas drikker te.
- en: Mina drinks coffee, and Jonas drinks tea.
:::

Both facts are added.

::: examples
- no: Mina drikker kaffe, men Jonas drikker te.
- en: Mina drinks coffee, but Jonas drinks tea.
:::

The drinks are presented as a contrast.

::: examples
- no: Mina drikker kaffe, eller Jonas drikker te.
- en: Mina drinks coffee, or Jonas drinks tea.
:::

The sentence presents the clauses as alternatives.

::: examples
- no: Mina drikker kaffe, for hun er trøtt.
- en: Mina drinks coffee because she is tired.
:::

The second clause gives Mina’s reason.

::: examples
- no: Mina er trøtt, så hun drikker kaffe.
- en: Mina is tired, so she drinks coffee.
:::

The second clause gives the result.

When two complete clauses are connected in the patterns taught here, a comma is normally required at the boundary:

::: examples
- no: Det er kaldt, men vi sitter ute.
- en: It is cold, but we are sitting outside.
- no: Vi sitter inne, for det regner.
- en: We are sitting inside because it is raining.
:::

Do not normally insert a comma when you are only connecting individual words:

::: examples
- no: Vi kjøper kaffe og kake.
- en: We buy coffee and cake.
:::

Meaning remains the main focus. First choose the correct relationship; then write the joined sentence clearly.

**Request: Name the Relationship**

**Purpose:** Consolidate the five meanings before freer sentence building.

**Context:** Short connected statements about Mina and Jonas at the café.

**Learner action:** For each joined sentence, identify whether the connection expresses addition, contrast, choice, reason, or result.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates accurate understanding of the meaning carried by each target conjunction. Production and punctuation are not scored in this activity.

{{exercise: name_the_relationship}}

**Request: Join Two Simple Clauses**

**Purpose:** Practise selecting and using all five target forms in controlled sentences.

**Context:** Pairs of clauses about ordinary plans, preferences, weather, food, and transport.

**Learner action:** Connect each pair with the conjunction that expresses the stated relationship, preserving both original ideas.

**Success meaning:** A successful response shows correct conjunction choice and a natural connection between two simple clauses.

{{exercise: join_two_simple_clauses}}

## Back at the café {#back-at-the-cafe role=model objectives=obj-coordinating-conjunctions-01}

On Saturday morning, Mina arrives first. Jonas sends her a message.

::: {.reading translation="The bus is delayed, so I will be a little late." dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Bussen er forsinket, så jeg kommer litt sent.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That is fine because I have a book with me." dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Det går bra, for jeg har en bok med meg.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Do you want to wait inside or outside?" dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Vil du vente inne eller ute?
:::

::: {.reading translation="I am sitting inside and reading." dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Jeg sitter inne og leser.
:::

::: {.reading translation="It is raining, but I have an umbrella." dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Det regner, men jeg har paraply.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good! I will order coffee, and you can order when you arrive." dialogue_id="cafe-update" speaker_id="mina" speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Fint! Jeg bestiller kaffe, og du kan bestille når du kommer.
:::

The same five words now help Mina and Jonas handle a changed situation. Notice how each connection has a distinct job:

- Mina waits and reads: addition.
- Jonas offers two places to wait: choice.
- The bus delay causes him to arrive late: result.
- Mina explains why waiting is acceptable: reason.
- Rain creates a problem, but the umbrella changes the expectation: contrast.

**Request: Reconstruct the Café Update**

**Purpose:** Return to the chapter’s characters and integrate all five meanings.

**Context:** Mina is waiting at the café while Jonas’s bus is delayed.

**Learner action:** Reconstruct short messages by joining the provided words or clauses with **og, men, eller, for,** or **så**, according to the intended meaning.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates controlled selection of all five conjunctions within one coherent situation.
