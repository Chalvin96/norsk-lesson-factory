## At the apartment viewing {#at-the-apartment-viewing role=orient}

Ingrid and Jonas are viewing an apartment with Mari, the estate agent. The building is quiet, but several small details make them wonder what living there would be like.

::: {.reading translation="Welcome in. Just watch out for the sleeping cat by the door." dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=mari speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Velkommen inn. Bare pass på den sovende katten ved døra.
:::

::: {.reading translation="It doesn’t look particularly dangerous." dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name="Ingrid" character_id="ingrid"}
Den ser ikke særlig farlig ut.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Did you hear the beeping sound?" dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Hørte dere den pipende lyden?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, it’s a smoke alarm in the hallway. The caretaker comes running as soon as anything beeps." dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=mari speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Ja, det er en røykvarsler i gangen. Vaktmesteren kommer løpende så snart noe piper.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Who lives on the floor above?" dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=ingrid speaker_name="Ingrid" character_id="ingrid"}
Hvem bor i etasjen over?
:::

::: {.reading translation="The people who live above you are an older couple. They often have a smiling grandchild visiting, but otherwise it’s quiet." dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=mari speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
De som bor over dere, er et eldre par. De har ofte et smilende barnebarn på besøk, men ellers er det stille.
:::

::: {.reading translation="And the man who came carrying the large box?" dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Og mannen som kom bærende på den store esken?
:::

::: {.reading translation="That was the neighbour. He is moving out this week." dialogue_id=apartment_viewing speaker_id=mari speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Det var naboen. Han flytter ut denne uka.
:::

The English translations contain several forms ending in **-ing**, but Norwegian does not handle all of them in the same way. Some become Norwegian **-ende** forms:

::: examples
- no: den sovende katten
- en: the sleeping cat
- no: den pipende lyden
- en: the beeping sound
- no: Vaktmesteren kommer løpende.
- en: The caretaker comes running.
- no: Han kom bærende på en eske.
- en: He came carrying a box.
:::

Other English -ing expressions need a finite verb or another structure:

::: examples
- no: De som bor over dere
- en: The people living above you
- no: Han flytter ut denne uka.
- en: He is moving out this week.
:::

The important question is therefore not simply, “How do I translate -ing?” It is, “What job is the English -ing form doing, and what sounds natural in Norwegian?”

## The present participle {#the-present-participle role=model objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

Forms such as **sovende**, **pipende**, **løpende**, and **bærende** are examples of the **present participle (presens partisipp)**. A present participle normally ends in **-ende**.

For an infinitive ending in an unstressed **-e**, remove that final **-e** and add **-ende**:

::: examples
- no: å sove → sovende
- en: to sleep → sleeping
- no: å pipe → pipende
- en: to beep → beeping
- no: å smile → smilende
- en: to smile → smiling
- no: å bære → bærende
- en: to carry → carrying
:::

For the ordinary verb patterns illustrated here, if the infinitive does not end in an unstressed **-e**, add **-ende** directly:

::: examples
- no: å gå → gående
- en: to walk → walking
- no: å bo → boende
- en: to live → living
- no: å le → leende
- en: to laugh → laughing
:::

The present participle presents an activity, reaction, or state as a characteristic or accompanying circumstance. It does not by itself tell us when the action happens. Time comes from the surrounding sentence.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jeg så en gråtende gutt.
- en: I saw a crying boy.
- no: Jeg ser en gråtende gutt.
- en: I see a crying boy.
:::

The form **gråtende** remains the same. The verbs **så** and **ser** place the scene in past and present time.

**Context:** Short descriptions from an apartment building, including people, animals, sounds, and movement.

**Learner action:** Locate the **-ende** forms and connect each one to the person, thing, or event it describes.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates recognition of present participles in meaningful sentences. Whether each form is adjective-like or adverb-like is supporting context, not the primary scored distinction here.

## Adjective-like use: describing someone or something {#adjective-like-use role=model objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

A present participle can be **adjective-like**: it gives information about a noun.

::: examples
- no: en sovende katt
- en: a sleeping cat
- no: et sovende barn
- en: a sleeping child
- no: sovende passasjerer
- en: sleeping passengers
:::

The participle **sovende** is **invariable**. This means it does not change for gender, definiteness, or number. Compare it with an ordinary adjective:

::: examples
- no: en rolig katt
- en: a calm cat
- no: et rolig barn
- en: a calm child
- no: rolige passasjerer
- en: calm passengers
:::

The adjective changes from **rolig** to **rolige** in the plural, but the participle remains **sovende**.

The same pattern appears with definite noun phrases:

::: examples
- no: den sovende katten
- en: the sleeping cat
- no: det sovende barnet
- en: the sleeping child
- no: de sovende passasjerene
- en: the sleeping passengers
:::

Only the article and noun change. The present participle stays in the same form.

More natural examples include:

::: examples
- no: Vi våknet av en pipende alarm.
- en: We woke up because of a beeping alarm.
- no: De bar ut det brennende søppelet.
- en: They carried out the burning rubbish.
- no: Hun prøvde å roe de gråtende barna.
- en: She tried to calm the crying children.
- no: Kokende vann kan gi alvorlige brannskader.
- en: Boiling water can cause serious burns.
:::

Some common **-ende** forms function much like established adjectives:

::: examples
- no: Det var en spennende visning.
- en: It was an exciting viewing.
- no: De diskuterte et overraskende tilbud.
- en: They discussed a surprising offer.
- no: Dette er de gjeldende reglene.
- en: These are the current applicable rules.
:::

These forms remain invariable too:

::: examples
- no: en spennende film
- en: an exciting film
- no: et spennende tilbud
- en: an exciting offer
- no: spennende muligheter
- en: exciting opportunities
:::

**Compact rule**

::: {.rule}
Use an adjective-like present participle when an activity, reaction, or ongoing state naturally characterizes a person or thing. Keep the **-ende** form unchanged.
:::

**Checkpoint request: keep-ende-forms-invariable**

**Purpose:** Prevent adjective agreement from being added to a present participle.

**Context:** Descriptions of one person, one neuter-gender object, and several people or objects around the apartment building.

**Learner action:** Supply the same appropriate **-ende** form in noun phrases with different genders and numbers.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner keeps the participle unchanged while allowing articles and nouns to carry the necessary grammatical distinctions.

## When a relative clause sounds more natural {#relative-clause role=contrast objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

An English noun phrase with -ing can sometimes use a Norwegian present participle:

::: examples
- no: en sovende hund
- en: a sleeping dog
:::

However, Norwegian often prefers a relative clause with **som** when the English phrase identifies a person or group by what they regularly do or where they live.

In the dialogue, Mari says:

::: examples
- no: De som bor over dere, er et eldre par.
- en: The people living above you are an older couple.
:::

It is possible to encounter **de boende** in formal language, where it means the residents. In ordinary conversation, however, **de som bor der** or **folk som bor der** is usually more natural.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Folk som jobber hjemmefra, trenger ofte et rolig rom.
- en: People working from home often need a quiet room.
- no: Studentene som venter utenfor, skal på visning.
- en: The students waiting outside are going to the viewing.
- no: Kvinnen som snakker med Mari, er Ingrid.
- en: The woman speaking with Mari is Ingrid.
:::

A present participle may sound compressed, descriptive, literary, or formal where a relative clause sounds clearer and more conversational.

Compare these two ways of viewing the same scene:

::: examples
- no: Jeg så en mann som bar på en stor eske.
- en: I saw a man who was carrying a large box.
- no: Jeg så en mann bærende på en stor eske.
- en: I saw a man carrying a large box.
:::

The second sentence is possible, but the first is often the neutral everyday choice. Norwegian uses **-ende** selectively rather than automatically.

**Checkpoint request: choose-participle-or-relative-clause**

**Purpose:** Develop natural judgment when an English noun-modifying -ing form does not require a Norwegian participle.

**Context:** Everyday descriptions of neighbours, residents, visitors, and workers in and around a building.

**Learner action:** Reformulate English-inspired descriptions using either a natural **-ende** modifier or a relative clause with **som**, according to the intended meaning and register.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates a natural structural choice. The activity scores the choice between a participle and a relative clause; exact vocabulary is secondary.

## Adverb-like use: an accompanying action or manner {#adverb-like-use role=model objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

A present participle can also be **adverb-like**. It then describes how someone moves, arrives, reacts, speaks, or performs another action.

In the dialogue:

::: examples
- no: Vaktmesteren kommer løpende.
- en: The caretaker comes running.
:::

The main verb is **kommer**. The participle **løpende** tells us how he comes.

::: examples
- no: Naboen kom bærende på en stor eske.
- en: The neighbour came carrying a large box.
:::

The main event is his arrival. **Bærende på en stor eske** describes his condition or accompanying activity when he arrived.

This pattern is particularly common with movement and position:

::: examples
- no: Hun kom syklende ned bakken.
- en: She came cycling down the hill.
- no: Barna løp leende ut i hagen.
- en: The children ran laughing into the garden.
- no: Han gikk haltende bort til bilen.
- en: He walked limping over to the car.
- no: Vi ble stående utenfor døra.
- en: We remained standing outside the door.
- no: De ble sittende og snakke etter møtet.
- en: They remained sitting and talking after the meeting.
:::

Present participles can also describe manner:

::: examples
- no: Hun svarte nølende.
- en: She answered hesitantly.
- no: Han så spørrende på meg.
- en: He looked at me questioningly.
- no: Ingrid nikket smilende til Mari.
- en: Ingrid nodded to Mari with a smile.
:::

These sentences do not use the participle to create an English-style progressive tense. The sentence already has a main verb: **kom**, **løp**, **gikk**, **svarte**, **så**, or **nikket**. The participle adds an accompanying action, posture, expression, or manner.

**Meaning contrast**

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jonas løp til bussen.
- en: Jonas ran to the bus.
- no: Jonas kom løpende til bussen.
- en: Jonas came running to the bus.
:::

The first sentence presents running as the main action. The second presents arrival or movement toward the scene as the main event and running as the manner.

Another contrast:

::: examples
- no: Mari smilte til Ingrid.
- en: Mari smiled at Ingrid.
- no: Mari svarte smilende.
- en: Mari answered with a smile.
:::

In the first sentence, smiling is the main action. In the second, answering is the main action, while **smilende** describes Mari’s manner or expression.

**Checkpoint request: use-participle-for-accompanying-action**

**Purpose:** Practise adverb-like participles without confusing them with finite verbs.

**Context:** People entering a building, carrying objects, reacting to news, or speaking during a viewing.

**Learner action:** Add an appropriate invariable **-ende** form to a sentence that already contains its main verb.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner can express an accompanying action, posture, or manner while retaining a complete finite main verb.

## Norwegian does not normally have an English-style progressive {#norwegian-no-progressive role=contrast objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

English frequently uses **be + -ing** to describe an activity in progress:

English example: Ingrid is looking at the kitchen.

Norwegian normally uses an ordinary present-tense verb:

::: examples
- no: Ingrid ser på kjøkkenet.
- en: Ingrid is looking at the kitchen.
:::

A mechanical translation with **er + present participle** is usually wrong:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Ingrid er seende på kjøkkenet.
- en: Ingrid is looking at the kitchen.
:::

This does not sound natural in the intended meaning.

More contrasts:

::: examples
- no: Jeg lager middag nå.
- en: I am making dinner now.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg er lagende middag nå.
- en: I am making dinner now.
:::

::: examples
- no: De diskuterer prisen.
- en: They are discussing the price.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ De er diskuterende prisen.
- en: They are discussing the price.
:::

::: examples
- no: Barnet sover.
- en: The child is sleeping.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Barnet er sovende.
- en: The child is sleeping.
:::

The adjective-like phrase **et sovende barn** is natural because **sovende** describes the noun **barn**. In the complete statement **Barnet sover**, however, Norwegian normally uses the finite verb **sover**.

This gives an important contrast:

::: examples
- no: Det ligger et sovende barn i vogna.
- en: There is a sleeping child lying in the pram.
- no: Barnet sover i vogna.
- en: The child is sleeping in the pram.
:::

The first describes the child within a larger scene. The second directly states what the child is doing.

**Common misconception**

An **-ende** form is not simply the Norwegian equivalent of every English -ing form. Norwegian has present participles, but it does not normally build an ongoing tense by combining **å være** with them.

Use the ordinary present or past tense when the activity itself is the main statement:

::: examples
- no: Jeg venter på bussen.
- en: I am waiting for the bus.
- no: Jeg ventet på bussen da hun ringte.
- en: I was waiting for the bus when she called.
:::

Use an **-ende** form when it describes a noun or accompanies another main event:

::: examples
- no: De ventende passasjerene så på klokka.
- en: The waiting passengers looked at the clock.
- no: Hun kom løpende til bussholdeplassen.
- en: She came running to the bus stop.
:::

**Checkpoint request: replace-false-progressive-forms**

**Purpose:** Break the habit of translating English **be + -ing** as Norwegian **være + -ende**.

**Context:** Short updates about what Ingrid, Jonas, Mari, and the neighbours are doing now or were doing at a particular moment.

**Learner action:** Replace unnatural progressive-style constructions with natural finite Norwegian verbs.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates that the learner identifies the activity as the main predicate and uses an ordinary present- or past-tense verb rather than **være + -ende**.

{{exercise: replace_false_progressive_forms}}

## Other English -ing forms often need other Norwegian structures {#other-english-ing-structures role=contrast objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

English -ing appears in several grammatical jobs. Norwegian chooses a structure according to meaning.

**An activity as a general idea**

English can make an activity the subject of a sentence with an -ing form: Cycling is good exercise.

Norwegian usually uses an infinitive:

::: examples
- no: Det er god trening å sykle.
- en: Cycling is good exercise.
- no: Å lese kontrakten nøye er viktig.
- en: Reading the contract carefully is important.
- no: Det er dyrt å bo alene.
- en: Living alone is expensive.
:::

Do not mechanically use **syklende**, **lesende**, or **boende** for this meaning.

**Two events happening at the same time**

English can begin with an -ing phrase: Walking home, Ingrid called Jonas.

Norwegian can often use **mens** or another ordinary clause:

::: examples
- no: Mens Ingrid gikk hjem, ringte hun Jonas.
- en: While Ingrid was walking home, she called Jonas.
- no: På vei hjem ringte Ingrid til Jonas.
- en: On her way home, Ingrid called Jonas.
- no: Gående hjemover tenkte Ingrid på leiligheten.
- en: Walking home, Ingrid thought about the apartment.
:::

A natural phrase such as **på vei hjem** may also fit the intended meaning:

::: examples
- no: På vei hjem ringte Ingrid til Jonas.
- en: On her way home, Ingrid called Jonas.
:::

A present participle is possible in some descriptive contexts, but it can sound more written or compressed:

::: examples
- no: Gående hjemover tenkte Ingrid på leiligheten.
- en: Walking home, Ingrid thought about the apartment.
:::

In ordinary conversation, **mens hun gikk hjem** or **på vei hjem** will often be more natural.

**Method or means**

English often uses -ing after **by**: You open the door by pressing the green button.

Norwegian commonly uses **ved å** plus the infinitive:

::: examples
- no: Du åpner døra ved å trykke på den grønne knappen.
- en: You open the door by pressing the green button.
- no: De sparer penger ved å dele på utgiftene.
- en: They save money by sharing the expenses.
:::

Again, an **-ende** form is not the automatic solution.

**Structure-choice summary**

When you meet an English -ing form, ask what it does:

- If it naturally describes a noun, an invariable Norwegian **-ende** form may fit.
- If it adds manner or an accompanying action to a main event, an adverb-like **-ende** form may fit.
- If it is the main activity in progress, use a finite Norwegian verb.
- If it identifies people by what they do, a relative clause with **som** is often natural.
- If it names an activity in general, use an infinitive.
- If it means while doing something or by doing something, use a clause or an infinitive structure that expresses that relationship clearly.

**Checkpoint request: select-natural-structure-for-english-ing**

**Purpose:** Practise meaning-based translation instead of replacing every English -ing form with **-ende**.

**Context:** Connected statements about viewing an apartment, travelling home, reading a contract, contacting neighbours, and reducing living costs.

**Learner action:** Translate or reformulate each English -ing expression using the natural Norwegian structure required by its role.

**Success meaning:** A successful response demonstrates appropriate selection among an **-ende** form, a finite verb, a relative clause, or an infinitive-based expression. The scored goal is structural naturalness, not mastery of those alternative structures as separate grammar topics.

## Common mistakes to watch for {#common-mistakes role=contrast objectives=obj-present-participle-01}

**Changing the participle for gender or number**

Incorrect pattern:

::: examples
- no: et sovendt barn
- en: 'Incorrect: a sleeping child'
:::

Natural form:

::: examples
- no: et sovende barn
- en: a sleeping child
:::

Correct form, despite possible learner doubt:

::: examples
- no: de sovende barna
- en: the sleeping children
:::

Here, **sovende** does not need a plural ending. Learners sometimes try to add one because ordinary adjectives often change. Keep **-ende** unchanged.

**Using være + -ende for an action in progress**

Unnatural:

::: examples
- no: Vi er ventende på Mari.
- en: We are waiting for Mari.
:::

Natural:

::: examples
- no: Vi venter på Mari.
- en: We are waiting for Mari.
:::

But the participle can describe the people:

::: examples
- no: De ventende kjøperne begynte å bli utålmodige.
- en: The waiting buyers began to become impatient.
:::

**Forcing a participle into every noun phrase**

Possible but unnecessarily compressed:

::: examples
- no: menneskene boende i bygningen
- en: the people living in the building
:::

More natural in ordinary speech:

::: examples
- no: menneskene som bor i bygningen
- en: the people who live in the building
:::

**Confusing the main action with an accompanying action**

::: examples
- no: Hun løp ned trappa.
- en: She ran down the stairs.
:::

Here, running is the main action.

::: examples
- no: Hun kom løpende ned trappa.
- en: She came running down the stairs.
:::

Here, **kom** presents the main event, while **løpende** describes how she came.
