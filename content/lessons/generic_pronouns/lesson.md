## At the tourist office {#at-tourist-office role=orient objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

Nora and Elias work at a tourist office near a popular mountain trail. They are revising safety information for visitors. The facts are clear, but they disagree about how the advice should sound.

**Nora:**

::: {.reading translation="It says here: When you go hiking in the mountains, you must be prepared for sudden changes in the weather." dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Her står det: Når man går i fjellet, må man være forberedt på raske værskifter.
:::

**Elias:**

::: {.reading translation="That is correct, but a little formal. Can we write: When you go hiking in the mountains, you must be prepared?" dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="elias" speaker_name="Elias" character_id="elias"}
Det er korrekt, men litt formelt. Kan vi skrive: Når du går i fjellet, må du være forberedt?
:::

**Nora:**

::: {.reading translation="Yes, that sounds more direct. But some readers may think that we are speaking to them personally." dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Ja, det høres mer direkte ut. Men noen lesere kan tro at vi snakker til dem personlig.
:::

**Elias:**

::: {.reading translation="Perhaps that is the point. We are giving advice to whoever is reading." dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="elias" speaker_name="Elias" character_id="elias"}
Det er kanskje meningen. Vi gir jo råd til den som leser.
:::

**Nora:**

::: {.reading translation="True. Farther down it says that bad weather can make one uncertain. That is wrong, isn’t it?" dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Sant. Lenger nede står det at dårlig vær kan gjøre man usikker. Det er vel feil?
:::

**Elias:**

::: {.reading translation="Yes. Within an impersonal man/en reference, man can be used as the subject, but not as the object. We must write that bad weather can make one uncertain." dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="elias" speaker_name="Elias" character_id="elias"}
Ja. I en upersonlig man/en-referanse kan man bruke «man» som subjekt, men ikke som objekt. Vi må skrive at dårlig vær kan gjøre en usikker.
:::

**Nora:**

::: {.reading translation="And what about this sentence: People often forget that it gets colder in the evening?" dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="nora" speaker_name="Nora" character_id="nora"}
Og hva med denne setningen: Folk glemmer ofte at det blir kaldere om kvelden?
:::

**Elias:**

::: {.reading translation="That works if we mean that many visitors actually do it. Man glemmer ofte sounds more like a general human tendency." dialogue_id="at-tourist-office" speaker_id="elias" speaker_name="Elias" character_id="elias"}
Den passer hvis vi mener at mange besøkende faktisk gjør det. Man glemmer ofte høres mer ut som en generell menneskelig tendens.
:::

Nora keeps all four expressions, but she uses them in different parts of the information sheet. The choice is not only about grammar. It also changes the tone and the kind of generalization being made.

## What are Nora and Elias trying to say? {#what-are-nora-and-elias-trying-to-say role=orient objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

Norwegian has several ways to refer to people in general without naming one particular person:

- **man** presents something as generally true of any person in the situation.
- **en** can have a similar generic meaning and supplies the non-subject form within an impersonal **man/en** reference.
- **du** addresses the reader or listener directly but can still mean anyone in that situation.
- **folk** means people as a group, often a real or imaginable group rather than an abstract individual.

The central question is therefore:

Are you describing what any person typically does, involving the listener directly, or talking about people as a group—and what grammatical role does the expression have?

## **Man** for a general person {#man-for-a-general-person role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

Generic **man** is common when stating a general rule, experience, expectation, or human tendency. Grammatically, it functions as a subject.

::: examples
- no: Man lærer mye av å gjøre feil.
- en: You learn a lot from making mistakes.
- no: Man må ha billett for å ta toget.
- en: You need a ticket to take the train.
- no: I Norge tar man ofte av seg skoene når man kommer inn.
- en: In Norway, people often take off their shoes when they come inside.
:::

English often translates **man** with generic you, people, one, or a passive construction. Do not expect one fixed English equivalent.

::: examples
- no: Her kan man kjøpe mat hele døgnet.
- en: Food can be bought here around the clock.
:::

The Norwegian sentence literally presents a general person who can buy food. Natural English may instead use a passive form.

Although **man** refers broadly to people, it is grammatically singular:

::: examples
- no: Man må ta ansvar for det man gjør.
- en: One must take responsibility for what one does.
:::

Modern Bokmål finite verbs do not distinguish singular and plural, so **må** does not demonstrate number here. The relative clause uses **man** separately because **man** is also the embedded clause’s generic subject.

## Tone of **man** {#tone-of-man role=contrast objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

**Man** often sounds neutral and somewhat detached. It is useful in explanations, general observations, rules, and informational writing.

::: examples
- no: Hvis man bestiller tidlig, får man som regel en bedre pris.
- en: If you book early, you usually get a better price.
:::

The speaker is not necessarily advising one specific listener. The sentence describes a general consequence.

**Man** is not automatically stiff or old-fashioned, but repeated use can make a text sound impersonal. That is why Elias suggests **du** for advice addressed directly to tourists.

## Sentence role: **man** cannot be an object {#sentence-role-man-cannot-be-an-object role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

This is the grammatical problem Nora notices in the draft.

::: examples
- no: Dårlig vær kan gjøre en usikker.
- en: Bad weather can make you uncertain.
:::

Here, bad weather is the subject, and the affected person is the object. Within the intended impersonal **man/en** reference, Norwegian uses **en**, not **man**, for that object.

::: examples
- no: Slike kommentarer kan gjøre en nervøs.
- en: Comments like that can make one nervous.
- no: Ingen liker å bli behandlet som om en ikke forstår noe.
- en: No one likes to be treated as though one does not understand anything.
:::

In the second example, **en** is the subject of the subordinate clause after **som om**. It still refers to a generic person.

Compare the roles:

::: examples
- no: Man blir lett sliten i sterk varme.
- en: You easily become tired in intense heat.
- no: Sterk varme kan gjøre en sliten.
- en: Intense heat can make you tired.
:::

In the first sentence, the generic person is the subject, so **man** is possible. In the second, the generic person is the object of **gjøre** within the same impersonal **man/en** reference, so **en** is required.

A compact rule is:

::: {.rule}
Within an impersonal **man/en** reference, use **man** only as a generic subject and **en** as the object form. **En** may also appear as a generic subject, depending on style. Generic **du** instead has object form **deg**, while the noun **folk** remains **folk** when used as an object.
:::

A common mistake is to treat **man** as if it had the same form in every position.

::: examples
- no: Denne erfaringen lærer en å være forsiktig.
- en: This experience teaches one to be careful.
:::

Not:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Denne erfaringen lærer man å være forsiktig.
- en: This experience teaches one to be careful.
:::

The rejected version does not express the intended object meaning. It instead encourages the reader to parse **man** as the subject of what follows.

## Generic **en** as a subject {#generic-en-as-a-subject role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

**En** can also replace subject **man**:

::: examples
- no: En lærer mye av å gjøre feil.
- en: One learns a lot from making mistakes.
- no: Når en bor alene, må en planlegge økonomien nøye.
- en: When one lives alone, one has to plan one’s finances carefully.
:::

This use is established Bokmål, though preferences vary by speaker, region, and style. In many contexts, **man** is the more immediately neutral subject choice. Some speakers consistently prefer **en**, especially when they wish to avoid **man** or maintain the same generic reference across a passage.

The important grammatical contrast within this impersonal pattern is not that **en** is always an object. It is that **man** is restricted to subject position, while **en** is not.

## Recognition note: referring back to the same generic person {#recognition-note-referring-back-to-the-same-generic-person role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

For recognition only, **seg** in the examples below refers back to the generic subject.

::: examples
- no: Man må beskytte seg mot sola.
- en: You have to protect yourself from the sun.
- no: En bør spørre seg om turen passer for alle.
- en: One should ask oneself whether the trip is suitable for everyone.
:::

The possessive forms in the following examples also refer back to the generic subject; choosing among those forms is outside this lesson’s objective.

::: examples
- no: Man må kjenne sine egne grenser.
- en: You have to know your own limits.
- no: En bør ta med seg maten sin.
- en: One should bring one’s food along.
:::

These forms are reference cues only. They do not turn **man** into an object and are not assessed here.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Man må ta vare på seg selv.
- en: One must take care of oneself.
- no: Andre kan hjelpe en.
- en: Other people can help one.
:::

In the first sentence, **seg selv** refers back to the subject **man**. In the second, **en** is the object of **hjelpe** within an impersonal **man/en** reference.

These reference forms remain supporting context only; no reflexive or possessive choice is requested or scored.

## **Du** for direct, involving generalization {#du-for-direct-involving-generalization role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

Generic **du** is grammatically the ordinary second-person pronoun, but it does not always refer only to one specific listener. It can mean anyone who finds themself in the described situation.

::: examples
- no: Når du flytter til et nytt sted, tar det tid å bli kjent med folk.
- en: When you move to a new place, it takes time to get to know people.
:::

The speaker is not necessarily claiming that the listener is moving. The sentence invites the listener to imagine the general experience.

Generic **du** is especially common in conversation, advice, instructions, and accessible public information.

::: examples
- no: Hvis du tar bussen etter midnatt, må du kjøpe billetten på forhånd.
- en: If you take the bus after midnight, you have to buy the ticket in advance.
:::

Compare the same basic information:

::: examples
- no: Hvis man tar bussen etter midnatt, må man kjøpe billetten på forhånd.
- en: If one takes the bus after midnight, one must buy the ticket in advance.
- no: Hvis du tar bussen etter midnatt, må du kjøpe billetten på forhånd.
- en: If you take the bus after midnight, you have to buy the ticket in advance.
:::

The **man** version presents a neutral general rule. The **du** version turns toward the reader and makes the advice feel more immediate.

This does not mean that **du** is always friendlier. In criticism or discussion of sensitive behavior, generic **du** may sound as though the speaker is accusing the actual listener.

::: examples
- no: Du blir fort irritert når du ikke får det som du vil.
- en: You quickly become irritated when you do not get what you want.
:::

Even if the speaker intends a general observation, the listener may hear a personal judgment.

A less personal version is:

::: examples
- no: Man blir fort irritert når man ikke får det som man vil.
- en: People quickly become irritated when they do not get what they want.
:::

The choice therefore depends on communicative distance. Use **du** when involving the addressee is useful. Use **man** when the statement should remain detached from the particular listener.

## **Folk** for people as a group {#folk-for-people-as-a-group role=model objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

**Folk** is a noun meaning people. It commonly refers to a group, category, or population rather than to an abstract representative person.

::: examples
- no: Folk spør ofte hvor lang turen er.
- en: People often ask how long the hike is.
- no: Mange folk undervurderer hvor raskt været kan skifte.
- en: Many people underestimate how quickly the weather can change.
- no: Vi prøver å gi folk tydelig informasjon.
- en: We try to give people clear information.
:::

Unlike **man**, **folk** can be either subject or object without changing form:

::: examples
- no: Folk trenger tydelige råd.
- en: People need clear advice.
- no: Tydelige råd hjelper folk.
- en: Clear advice helps people.
:::

The meaning also differs slightly from **man**. Compare:

::: examples
- no: Man glemmer lett tiden når man har det hyggelig.
- en: You easily lose track of time when you are enjoying yourself.
- no: Folk glemmer ofte tiden på denne kafeen.
- en: People often lose track of time in this café.
:::

The first sounds like a general human tendency. The second suggests an observed pattern among actual café customers.

Similarly:

::: examples
- no: Man bruker mindre strøm hvis man senker temperaturen.
- en: You use less electricity if you lower the temperature.
- no: Folk bruker mindre strøm enn før.
- en: People use less electricity than before.
:::

The **man** sentence describes what happens to any person under a condition. The **folk** sentence makes a claim about people collectively.

Do not assume that **folk** is simply a more informal substitute for **man**. It changes the perspective from a representative individual to a group.

## Returning to Nora and Elias {#returning-to-nora-and-elias role=recap objectives=obj-generic-pronouns-01}

Nora now reads the revised safety notice aloud.

::: examples
- no: Når du går i fjellet, må du være forberedt på raske værskifter. Ta med klær som beskytter deg mot vind og regn.
- en: When you go hiking in the mountains, you must be prepared for sudden changes in the weather. Bring clothes that protect you from wind and rain.
:::

Here **du** is intentional. The notice addresses each reader directly, and **deg** continues that direct second-person reference.

The next paragraph explains a broader principle:

::: examples
- no: Man bør alltid undersøke værmeldingen før man drar. Dårlig vær kan gjøre en usikker, og man må kjenne sine egne grenser.
- en: One should always check the weather forecast before leaving. Bad weather can make one uncertain, and one must know one’s own limits.
:::

Here **man** provides generic subjects, **en** is the object of **gjøre** within the impersonal **man/en** reference, and **sine** points back to the generic subject as a recognition cue only.

Finally, the office reports a pattern it has observed:

::: examples
- no: Folk spør ofte om de kan gå turen uten kart.
- en: People often ask whether they can do the hike without a map.
:::

This is about actual visitors as a group, so **folk** is natural.

The forms are not competing labels for exactly the same thing. Each choice contributes something:

Within an impersonal **man/en** reference, **man** gives a subject-only form and **en** supplies the object form while also being possible as a subject. **Du** involves the addressee and uses **deg** outside subject position, while the noun **folk** refers to people collectively and remains **folk** as an object.
