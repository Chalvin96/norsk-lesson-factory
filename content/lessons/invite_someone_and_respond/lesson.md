## A plan for Saturday {#a-plan-for-saturday role=orient objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Lea and Amir work together. On Friday afternoon, Lea wants to make plans for the weekend.

::: {.reading translation="Would you like to have a coffee tomorrow?" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="lea" speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Har du lyst til å ta en kaffe i morgen?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, I’d love to! When works for you?" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="amir" speaker_name="Amir" character_id="amir"}
Ja, gjerne! Når passer det?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Does two o’clock work?" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="lea" speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Passer klokka to?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Unfortunately, I can’t at two o’clock. I’m going to visit my sister." dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="amir" speaker_name="Amir" character_id="amir"}
Dessverre, jeg kan ikke klokka to. Jeg skal besøke søsteren min.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Oh, right. How about four o’clock?" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="lea" speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Å ja. Hva med klokka fire?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Four o’clock works well." dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="amir" speaker_name="Amir" character_id="amir"}
Klokka fire passer bra.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Great! Shall we meet at Café Sol?" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="lea" speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Flott! Skal vi møtes på Kafé Sol?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, let’s do that. See you on Saturday!" dialogue_id="a-plan-for-saturday" speaker_id="amir" speaker_name="Amir" character_id="amir"}
Ja, det gjør vi. Vi ses på lørdag!
:::

Lea makes an invitation, and Amir first accepts it. When the suggested time does not work, he declines that particular time courteously and explains why. Lea then offers an alternative. Together they find a plan that works.

The key is not one fixed sentence. A short social exchange often has three parts:

1. an invitation;
2. an acceptance or courteous decline;
3. an alternative if the original plan is not possible.

## Making an invitation {#making-an-invitation role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

## Har du lyst til å …? {#har-du-lyst-til-a role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

A common, friendly way to invite someone is:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å + infinitive?
- en: Would you like to + verb?
:::

The infinitive is the basic form of the verb, such as spise, gå, komme, or ta. After å, use this basic form.

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å spise lunsj sammen?
- en: Would you like to have lunch together?
- no: Har du lyst til å gå på kino på fredag?
- en: Would you like to go to the cinema on Friday?
- no: Har du lyst til å komme på besøk?
- en: Would you like to come over?
- no: Har du lyst til å ta en kaffe etter jobb?
- en: Would you like to have a coffee after work?
:::

In the dialogue, Lea says:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å ta en kaffe i morgen?
- en: Would you like to have a coffee tomorrow?
:::

Ta en kaffe literally contains the verb ta, “take,” but the whole expression normally means “have a coffee.” Learn it as a useful social expression.

Har du lyst til å …? asks whether the other person feels like doing something. It sounds friendly and leaves room for either answer.

## Vil du …? {#vil-du role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

You can also invite someone with vil du followed directly by the basic verb, without å.

::: examples
- no: Vil du spise middag med meg?
- en: Would you like to have dinner with me?
- no: Vil du bli med på konsert?
- en: Would you like to come along to a concert?
- no: Vil du komme på besøk på søndag?
- en: Would you like to come over on Sunday?
:::

Vil du …? can mean “Do you want to …?” or, in an invitation, the more natural English “Would you like to …?”

Notice the contrast:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å komme?
- en: Would you like to come?
- no: Vil du komme?
- en: Would you like to come?
:::

Both can be friendly invitations. The sentence pattern changes, however:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å komme?
- en: Would you like to come?
- no: Vil du komme?
- en: Would you like to come?
:::

After lyst, you need til å. After vil du, do not use å.

## Skal vi …? {#skal-vi role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

When you suggest doing something together, use skal vi followed directly by the basic verb.

::: examples
- no: Skal vi møtes på kafé?
- en: Shall we meet at a café?
- no: Skal vi spise sammen?
- en: Shall we eat together?
- no: Skal vi gå på kino i kveld?
- en: Shall we go to the cinema tonight?
:::

In the dialogue, Lea uses this form after she and Amir have agreed on a time:

::: examples
- no: Skal vi møtes på Kafé Sol?
- en: Shall we meet at Café Sol?
:::

Skal vi …? works well for suggesting a shared plan. Har du lyst til å …? and vil du …? focus more directly on inviting the other person.

## Checkpoint: Build the invitation {#checkpoint-build-the-invitation role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Create friendly invitations for three simple situations: having coffee tomorrow, going to the cinema on Friday, and eating lunch together. Use Har du lyst til å …? for at least one invitation, Vil du …? for another, and Skal vi …? for a shared suggestion.

This activity belongs here because it checks whether you can connect an activity to the correct invitation pattern. Keep the situations concrete, as Lea does when she mentions coffee and tomorrow. A successful response uses a complete, natural invitation and handles å correctly.

## Accepting an invitation {#accepting-an-invitation role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

A short ja is possible, but a warmer response shows enthusiasm.

::: examples
- no: Ja, gjerne!
- en: Yes, I’d love to!
- no: Ja, det vil jeg gjerne.
- en: Yes, I’d like that.
- no: Det høres hyggelig ut.
- en: That sounds nice.
- no: Det passer bra.
- en: That works well.
- no: Gjerne! Når passer det?
- en: I’d love to! When works for you?
:::

Gjerne is especially useful when accepting invitations and offers. Depending on context, it can correspond to “gladly,” “yes, please,” or “I’d love to.”

Compare these responses:

::: examples
- no: Vil du ta en kaffe?
- en: Would you like to have a coffee?
- no: Ja, gjerne!
- en: Yes, I’d love to!
:::

And:

::: examples
- no: Passer klokka fire?
- en: Does four o’clock work?
- no: Ja, det passer bra.
- en: Yes, that works well.
:::

The first response accepts an activity. The second confirms that a suggested time works.

You can also accept and ask for necessary details:

::: examples
- no: Ja, gjerne! Når?
- en: Yes, I’d love to! When?
- no: Det høres hyggelig ut. Hvor skal vi møtes?
- en: That sounds nice. Where shall we meet?
:::

These questions keep the exchange moving without requiring a long answer.

## Checkpoint: Respond warmly {#checkpoint-respond-warmly role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Imagine that Lea asks you to have coffee on Saturday. Give a warm acceptance, then ask when or where you should meet.

This follows the invitation practice because accepting involves more than recognizing the invitation. You now respond as a participant in the same kind of social situation. A successful response clearly accepts and adds one relevant question about the plan.

## Declining courteously {#declining-courteously role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Sometimes you cannot accept. A direct jeg kan ikke communicates the basic fact, but adding dessverre makes the refusal softer.

::: examples
- no: Dessverre, jeg kan ikke.
- en: Unfortunately, I can’t.
- no: Jeg kan dessverre ikke på fredag.
- en: Unfortunately, I can’t on Friday.
- no: Det passer dessverre ikke.
- en: Unfortunately, that doesn’t work.
- no: Beklager, jeg kan ikke i kveld.
- en: Sorry, I can’t tonight.
:::

In the dialogue, Amir does not reject Lea or the idea of meeting. He says that one time is impossible:

::: examples
- no: Dessverre, jeg kan ikke klokka to.
- en: Unfortunately, I can’t at two o’clock.
:::

He then gives a brief reason:

::: examples
- no: Jeg skal besøke søsteren min.
- en: I’m going to visit my sister.
:::

A reason can make a refusal sound considerate, but it does not need to be long.

::: examples
- no: Beklager, jeg kan ikke. Jeg jobber på lørdag.
- en: Sorry, I can’t. I’m working on Saturday.
- no: Det passer dessverre ikke. Jeg har en avtale.
- en: Unfortunately, that doesn’t work. I have an appointment.
- no: Jeg kan ikke i kveld. Jeg er veldig trøtt.
- en: I can’t tonight. I’m very tired.
:::

At A1 level, a simple courteous phrase is enough. You do not need to explain personal details.

## Offering an alternative {#offering-an-alternative role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

A good decline can keep the invitation open by offering another time or activity.

## Hva med …? {#hva-med role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Hva med …? means “How about …?” It can introduce an alternative time or day.

::: examples
- no: Hva med klokka fire?
- en: How about four o’clock?
- no: Hva med søndag?
- en: How about Sunday?
- no: Hva med neste uke?
- en: How about next week?
:::

You can combine a decline and an alternative:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan dessverre ikke på fredag. Hva med lørdag?
- en: Unfortunately, I can’t on Friday. How about Saturday?
- no: Klokka seks passer ikke. Hva med klokka sju?
- en: Six o’clock doesn’t work. How about seven?
:::

## Kan vi …? {#kan-vi role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Kan vi …? means “Can we …?” It can suggest a practical change.

::: examples
- no: Kan vi møtes på søndag?
- en: Can we meet on Sunday?
- no: Kan vi ta en kaffe neste uke?
- en: Can we have a coffee next week?
- no: Kan vi møtes litt senere?
- en: Can we meet a little later?
:::

## Another time works for me {#another-time-works-for-me role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

You can also state when you are available.

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan på lørdag.
- en: I can on Saturday.
- no: Jeg kan etter jobb.
- en: I can after work.
- no: Søndag passer bedre for meg.
- en: Sunday works better for me.
- no: Jeg kan ikke i kveld, men jeg kan i morgen.
- en: I can’t tonight, but I can tomorrow.
:::

The word men, “but,” makes it easy to connect the problem with an alternative:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan ikke klokka to, men jeg kan klokka fire.
- en: I can’t at two o’clock, but I can at four.
- no: Fredag passer ikke, men lørdag passer bra.
- en: Friday doesn’t work, but Saturday works well.
:::

This is often more helpful than ending with only a refusal.

## Choosing a response for the situation {#choosing-a-response-for-the-situation role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

An invitation may concern the activity, the time, or both. Listen or read carefully so that your answer addresses the right point.

## Accepting the activity {#accepting-the-activity role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å spise middag sammen?
- en: Would you like to have dinner together?
- no: Ja, gjerne!
- en: Yes, I’d love to!
:::

## Agreeing to a time {#agreeing-to-a-time role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

::: examples
- no: Passer søndag?
- en: Does Sunday work?
- no: Ja, søndag passer bra.
- en: Yes, Sunday works well.
:::

## Declining the activity {#declining-the-activity role=model objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

::: examples
- no: Vil du bli med på konsert?
- en: Would you like to come along to a concert?
- no: Beklager, jeg kan ikke.
- en: Sorry, I can’t.
:::

## Declining only the suggested time {#declining-only-the-suggested-time role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

::: examples
- no: Skal vi møtes klokka fem?
- en: Shall we meet at five?
- no: Klokka fem passer dessverre ikke. Hva med klokka seks?
- en: Unfortunately, five o’clock doesn’t work. How about six?
:::

In the final exchange, the speaker still wants to meet. Only the time changes. Making that distinction helps you respond naturally and avoids accidentally rejecting the whole invitation.

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

## Using å after vil {#using-a-after-vil role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Vil du å komme på besøk?
- en: Would you like to come over?
:::

Use the basic verb directly after vil:

::: examples
- no: Vil du komme på besøk?
- en: Would you like to come over?
:::

With har lyst, use til å:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å komme på besøk?
- en: Would you like to come over?
:::

## Leaving out til in har lyst til å {#leaving-out-til-in-har-lyst-til-a role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Har du lyst å ta en kaffe?
- en: Would you like to have a coffee?
:::

Natural Bokmål uses til å:

::: examples
- no: Har du lyst til å ta en kaffe?
- en: Would you like to have a coffee?
:::

## Giving an unclear acceptance {#giving-an-unclear-acceptance role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

If someone suggests a time, ja may be understandable, but a fuller response is clearer.

::: examples
- no: Ja, det passer bra.
- en: Yes, that works well.
:::

If someone invites you to an activity, gjerne sounds warm and natural.

::: examples
- no: Ja, gjerne!
- en: Yes, I’d love to!
:::

## Declining too abruptly {#declining-too-abruptly role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

This sentence is grammatically possible:

::: examples
- no: Nei, jeg kan ikke.
- en: No, I can’t.
:::

In a friendly invitation, a softer opening often sounds better:

::: examples
- no: Beklager, jeg kan ikke i kveld.
- en: Sorry, I can’t tonight.
- no: Dessverre, det passer ikke.
- en: Unfortunately, that doesn’t work.
:::

When you still want to meet, add an alternative:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan dessverre ikke i kveld. Hva med i morgen?
- en: Unfortunately, I can’t tonight. How about tomorrow?
:::

## Checkpoint: Repair the exchange {#checkpoint-repair-the-exchange role=contrast objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Take several short invitation exchanges and improve the responses. Where a response is abrupt, make it more courteous. Where it declines without continuing the conversation, add a suitable alternative. Where an invitation uses Har du lyst or Vil du, check the placement of å.

This checkpoint belongs here because it asks you to notice the most likely communication problems before producing a longer exchange. Use everyday contexts such as coffee, dinner, a film, or a visit. Successful revisions preserve the original meaning while making the invitation or response natural and socially clear.

## Extended practice: Complete a social exchange {#extended-practice-complete-a-social-exchange role=recap objectives=obj-invite-someone-and-respond-01}

Write a short exchange between two named people. One person invites the other to do an everyday activity at a particular time. The second person cannot accept that time but wants to accept the general invitation. They should decline courteously and offer an alternative. The first person should respond to that alternative and confirm the plan.

This activity returns to the full situation from the opening dialogue. It belongs near the end because it combines invitation, response, and alternative without introducing a new communicative goal. A successful exchange makes it clear what the activity is, why the first suggestion does not work, what the alternative is, and whether the final plan is accepted.

{{exercise: complete_social_exchange}}

Then reuse the same exchange with one change: let the invited person accept immediately. Adjust the response so that it sounds warm and asks for or confirms one practical detail. Success means you can choose between acceptance and courteous decline according to context rather than repeating one memorized answer.

{{exercise: accept_immediately}}
