## A missing key before the meeting {#a-missing-key-before-the-meeting role=orient objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

Maja and Jonas are preparing a meeting room. Sara has gone out to buy coffee, and the key seems to be missing.

::: {.reading translation="Do you have the key to the meeting room?" dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Har du nøkkelen til møterommet?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, I have it." dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, jeg har den.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good. I can’t find Sara—or, wait: I can’t find her." dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Bra. Jeg finner Sara ikke—eller, vent: Jeg finner henne ikke.
:::

::: {.reading translation="She is buying coffee. I just called her." dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Hun kjøper kaffe. Jeg ringte henne nettopp.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Did she answer?" dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Svarte hun?
:::

::: {.reading translation="No, she isn’t answering me." dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Nei, hun svarer meg ikke.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Can you open the meeting room now?" dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Kan du åpne møterommet nå?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, I can open it." dialogue_id="missing-key-before-the-meeting" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, jeg kan åpne det.
:::

The small words den, henne, meg and det prevent Maja and Jonas from repeating names and nouns. They are object pronouns (objektspronomen): pronouns used for the person or thing affected by an action.

The dialogue also shows that choosing the correct pronoun is only part of the job. Its position matters. Compare Maja’s self-correction:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner Sara ikke.
- en: I can’t find Sara.
- no: Jeg finner henne ikke.
- en: I can’t find her.
:::

A full noun such as Sara normally follows ikke in this kind of neutral sentence. A short, unstressed object pronoun such as henne normally moves in front of ikke when there is only one verb.

## What does the pronoun replace? {#what-does-the-pronoun-replace role=model objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

An object pronoun replaces a person, animal or thing already understood from the context.

::: examples
- no: Jeg ringer Sara.
- en: *I’m calling Sara.*
- no: Jeg ringer henne.
- en: *I’m calling her.*
:::

The second sentence uses henne because Sara is the object of ringer: she receives the call.

The common object forms are:

- meg for me
- deg for you, singular
- ham for him
- henne for her
- den for it when replacing a masculine or feminine noun
- det for it when replacing a neuter noun
- oss for us
- dere for you, plural
- dem for them

In Bokmål, han is also accepted as an object form in many contexts. This chapter uses ham because its form clearly distinguishes it from subject han.

Compare who performs the action and who receives it:

::: examples
- no: Hun ringer meg.
- en: *She is calling me.*
- no: Jeg ringer henne.
- en: *I am calling her.*
:::

In the first sentence, hun performs the action and meg receives it. In the second, jeg performs the action and henne receives it.

For things, the grammatical gender and number of the known noun guide the choice:

::: examples
- no: Har du nøkkelen? – Ja, jeg har den.
- en: *Do you have the key? – Yes, I have it.*
:::

Nøkkelen is masculine, so den replaces it.

::: examples
- no: Har du kortet? – Ja, jeg har det.
- en: *Do you have the card? – Yes, I have it.*
:::

Kortet is neuter, so det replaces it.

::: examples
- no: Har du koppene? – Ja, jeg har dem.
- en: *Do you have the cups? – Yes, I have them.*
:::

Koppene is plural, so dem replaces it.

**Request: Choose-the-known-object**

This activity belongs here because learners must identify the intended person or thing before deciding where to place the pronoun. Use short exchanges from the meeting-room scene involving Sara, Jonas, the key, the card and the cups. The learner replaces a repeated noun or name with an appropriate object pronoun. Success demonstrates correct pronoun choice from context; word order remains supplied and is not the scored dimension.

## Position in an affirmative sentence {#position-in-an-affirmative-sentence role=model objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

In a simple affirmative main clause with one verb, the object pronoun normally comes after the verb.

::: examples
- no: Maja ringer henne.
- en: *Maja is calling her.*
- no: Jonas hjelper meg.
- en: *Jonas is helping me.*
- no: Vi trenger det.
- en: *We need it.*
:::

A compact pattern is:

**subject + verb + object pronoun**

The pronoun stays connected to the verb even when another element begins the sentence. Norwegian main-clause word order places the verb early, but the object pronoun still follows the subject when the subject comes after that verb.

::: examples
- no: I dag ringer Maja henne.
- en: *Today Maja is calling her.*
- no: Etter møtet hjelper Jonas oss.
- en: *After the meeting Jonas will help us.*
- no: Nå trenger vi den.
- en: *Now we need it.*
:::

Do not place the pronoun before the verb by copying English questions or other familiar patterns.

::: examples
- no: Maja henne ringer.
- en: *Maja her calls.*
:::

This is not normal Norwegian word order. Use:

::: examples
- no: Maja ringer henne.
- en: *Maja is calling her.*
:::

**Request: Replace-and-place-affirmative**

Use affirmative statements about preparations for the meeting. Give the learner a sentence containing a repeated name or noun, then ask for a natural rewrite with an object pronoun. Include sentences beginning with the subject and sentences beginning with a time expression such as i dag or nå. The primary scored goal is placing the pronoun after the appropriate verb and subject structure. Correct pronoun choice supports the task but is not its main scored dimension.

{{exercise: replace_and_place_affirmative}}

## Object pronouns with ikke and one verb {#object-pronouns-with-ikke-and-one-verb role=contrast objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

The important contrast appears when a simple sentence contains ikke.

With a full noun object, Norwegian commonly places ikke before the object:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner ikke Sara.
- en: *I can’t find Sara.*
- no: Jonas har ikke nøkkelen.
- en: *Jonas doesn’t have the key.*
:::

With a short, unstressed object pronoun and only one verb, the pronoun normally comes before ikke:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner henne ikke.
- en: *I can’t find her.*
- no: Jonas har den ikke.
- en: *Jonas doesn’t have it.*
:::

Compare the pairs directly:

::: examples
- no: Maja kjenner ikke Jonas.
- en: *Maja doesn’t know Jonas.*
- no: Maja kjenner ham ikke.
- en: *Maja doesn’t know him.*
- no: Vi bruker ikke møterommet.
- en: *We aren’t using the meeting room.*
- no: Vi bruker det ikke.
- en: *We aren’t using it.*
- no: Sara hører ikke Maja.
- en: *Sara can’t hear Maja.*
- no: Sara hører henne ikke.
- en: *Sara can’t hear her.*
:::

The compact rule for a neutral sentence with one verb is:

**full noun: verb + ikke + noun**

**object pronoun: verb + pronoun + ikke**

This movement is sometimes called object shift, but the practical choice matters more than the label: if a short object pronoun replaces a known noun, put it before ikke in this one-verb pattern.

A common mistake is to assume that ikke always comes immediately after the verb.

::: examples
- no: Hun ser ikke meg.
- en: *She doesn’t see me.*
:::

This order can occur when meg is strongly contrasted or emphasized, as in not me, but someone else. For an ordinary neutral statement, use:

::: examples
- no: Hun ser meg ikke.
- en: *She doesn’t see me.*
:::

At this level, use the neutral pattern unless the context clearly demands strong contrast.

**Request: Move-before-ikke**

Return to Jonas trying to contact Sara. Present simple one-verb statements with full noun objects and ask the learner to replace those objects with pronouns while keeping the meaning negative. The learner must decide whether the pronoun belongs before or after ikke. Success demonstrates the neutral one-verb pattern: verb + object pronoun + ikke. Pronoun identity is supplied or made unambiguous so placement remains the primary scored goal.

{{exercise: move_before_ikke}}

**Request: Contrast-noun-and-pronoun**

Use aligned pairs about people and objects in the meeting room. One sentence retains a full noun; the other replaces it with a pronoun. Ask the learner to complete or rewrite the pair with natural placement around ikke. This activity belongs after the first negative practice because it tests whether the learner understands why noun and pronoun positions differ. Success demonstrates correct contrast between verb + ikke + noun and verb + pronoun + ikke.

{{exercise: contrast_noun_and_pronoun}}

## When the sentence has two verbs {#when-the-sentence-has-two-verbs role=contrast objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

Do not move every object pronoun in front of ikke. When a sentence has a modal verb plus an infinitive, the object belongs with the infinitive.

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan åpne det.
- en: *I can open it.*
- no: Jeg kan ikke åpne det.
- en: *I can’t open it.*
:::

Here, kan is followed by the main action åpne. The object det follows åpne.

::: examples
- no: Maja skal ringe henne.
- en: *Maja is going to call her.*
- no: Maja skal ikke ringe henne.
- en: *Maja is not going to call her.*
- no: Vi må hjelpe ham.
- en: *We must help him.*
- no: Vi må ikke forstyrre ham.
- en: *We must not disturb him.*
:::

The same principle applies when har is used with a past participle:

::: examples
- no: Jonas har sett henne.
- en: *Jonas has seen her.*
- no: Jonas har ikke sett henne.
- en: *Jonas hasn’t seen her.*
- no: De har funnet den.
- en: *They have found it.*
- no: De har ikke funnet den.
- en: *They haven’t found it.*
:::

Compare one verb and two verbs:

::: examples
- no: Jeg finner henne ikke.
- en: *I can’t find her.*
- no: Jeg kan ikke finne henne.
- en: *I can’t find her.*
:::

In the first sentence, finner is the only verb, so henne comes before ikke. In the second, henne is the object of finne and stays after that infinitive.

Another aligned contrast:

::: examples
- no: Hun ringer meg ikke.
- en: *She isn’t calling me.*
- no: Hun skal ikke ringe meg.
- en: *She isn’t going to call me.*
:::

The meaning is similar, but the verb structure changes the pronoun’s position.

The compact distinction is:

**one verb: verb + pronoun + ikke**

**two verbs: first verb + ikke + second verb + pronoun**

A frequent error is to apply the one-verb rule mechanically:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan henne ikke hjelpe.
- en: *I cannot her help.*
:::

Use:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan ikke hjelpe henne.
- en: *I can’t help her.*
:::

**Request: Count-the-verbs-and-place**

Use short negative statements about opening the room, calling Sara, finding the key and helping Jonas. Some sentences contain one verb; others contain a modal plus an infinitive or har plus a past participle. Ask the learner to place a supplied object pronoun naturally. The primary scored goal is choosing the correct position from the number and structure of the verbs. The activity succeeds when the learner distinguishes one-verb pronoun shift from two-verb placement after the main action.

{{exercise: count_the_verbs_and_place}}

**Request: Repair-the-message**

Present brief messages containing plausible placement errors, such as a pronoun left after ikke in a neutral one-verb sentence or moved before ikke in a two-verb sentence. Ask the learner to rewrite each complete sentence naturally without changing its meaning. Use only familiar meeting-room vocabulary so attention stays on placement. Success demonstrates recognition and correction of the two main error patterns.

{{exercise: repair_the_message}}

## Back in the meeting room {#back-in-the-meeting-room role=model objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

Sara returns with the coffee. Maja now has to work out what happened to the key and access card.

::: {.reading translation="Hi! Did you call me?" dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Hei! Ringte dere meg?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, Jonas called you, but you didn’t answer him." dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Ja, Jonas ringte deg, men du svarte ham ikke.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Sorry. I didn’t hear the phone—no, I didn’t hear it." dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Beklager. Jeg hørte telefonen ikke—nei, jeg hørte den ikke.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That’s fine. Now we can open the meeting room." dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Det går bra. Nå kan vi åpne møterommet.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Do you have the access card?" dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Har dere adgangskortet?
:::

::: {.reading translation="No, we haven’t found it." dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Nei, vi har ikke funnet det.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Wait! You gave it to me earlier, Maja. It’s in my pocket." dialogue_id="back-in-the-meeting-room" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Vent! Du ga meg det tidligere, Maja. Det ligger i lomma mi.
:::

The returning scene reuses all three decisions:

- deg, ham, den and det refer to known people or things.
- A one-verb negative sentence places the pronoun before ikke: du svarte ham ikke and jeg hørte den ikke.
- A two-verb negative sentence keeps the pronoun after the main action: vi har ikke funnet det.

## Cumulative transfer: preparing for a birthday visit {#cumulative-transfer-preparing-for-a-birthday-visit role=recap objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

Move to a new everyday situation. Nora and Emil are preparing to visit a friend. They need to find a present, contact their friend and bring some cakes.

**Request: Birthday-pronoun-selection**

Give short contextual sentences that repeatedly mention Nora, Emil, their friend, the present and the cakes. Ask the learner to replace one repeated person or thing with an appropriate object pronoun. Keep sentence positions already correct. The primary scored goal is pronoun choice, including person, noun gender and plural reference. Success demonstrates that the learner can identify what each pronoun replaces in a new context.

{{exercise: birthday_pronoun_selection}}

**Request: Birthday-pronoun-placement**

Use neutral affirmative and negative sentences from the birthday preparation. Include one-verb sentences and two-verb sentences, with the needed pronoun supplied. Ask the learner to produce a complete sentence with natural pronoun placement. The primary scored goal is position: after the verb in affirmative statements, before ikke in neutral one-verb negatives, and after the main action in two-verb negatives.

{{exercise: birthday_pronoun_placement}}

**Request: Birthday-integrated-rewrite**

Present a short connected account in which names and nouns are repeated unnaturally. Ask the learner to rewrite selected sentences using suitable object pronouns without changing the events. Include affirmative statements, one-verb negatives and two-verb negatives. Success demonstrates the full approved outcome: choosing and placing Norwegian object pronouns correctly in simple sentences.

{{exercise: birthday_integrated_rewrite}}

## Final retrieval {#final-retrieval role=recap objectives=obj-object-pronoun-position-01}

Without looking back, explain the placement difference among these three sentence types: an affirmative sentence, a negative sentence with one verb, and a negative sentence with two verbs. Then produce one Norwegian example of each using the same object pronoun. A successful review shows that you can retrieve the placement pattern and transfer it to a new everyday context.
