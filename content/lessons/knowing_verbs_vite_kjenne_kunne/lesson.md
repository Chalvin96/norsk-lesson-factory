## A first shift at the community centre {#a-first-shift-at-the-community-centre role=orient objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

Lea has recently moved to Trondheim. On Saturday she is helping at a neighbourhood event for the first time. Arne, one of the organisers, shows her around.

::: {.reading translation='Do you know where the kitchen is?' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='arne' speaker_name='Arne' voice_profile="masculine"}
Vet du hvor kjøkkenet er?
:::

::: {.reading translation='Yes, it’s in the corridor on the left. But I don’t know the building very well yet.' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Ja, det er i gangen til venstre. Men jeg kjenner ikke bygningen så godt ennå.
:::

::: {.reading translation='Do you know Mari? She is in charge of the food.' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='arne' speaker_name='Arne' voice_profile="masculine"}
Kjenner du Mari? Hun har ansvaret for maten.
:::

::: {.reading translation='No, but I know who she is. We haven’t spoken before.' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Nei, men jeg vet hvem hun er. Vi har ikke snakket sammen før.
:::

::: {.reading translation='Do you know how to make coffee with the large machine?' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='arne' speaker_name='Arne' voice_profile="masculine"}
Kan du lage kaffe med den store maskinen?
:::

::: {.reading translation='Yes, I know how to use that kind of machine. I used to work in a café.' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Ja, jeg kan bruke en slik maskin. Jeg jobbet på kafé før.
:::

::: {.reading translation='Great. Do you know when the guests are coming?' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='arne' speaker_name='Arne' voice_profile="masculine"}
Flott. Vet du når gjestene kommer?
:::

::: {.reading translation='They’re coming at two, I think.' dialogue_id='first-shift-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
De kommer klokka to, tror jeg.
:::

The English verb *know* appears several times in this conversation, but Norwegian does not use one verb in all these situations. Arne and Lea choose between **vite**, **kjenne**, and **kunne** according to the kind of knowledge they mean.

- **vite** is used for facts and information.
- **kjenne** is used for familiarity with a person, place, or thing.
- **kunne** is used for a learned skill or ability.

The common present-tense forms are:

::: examples
- no: vite → vet
- en: to know a fact → know/knows
:::

::: examples
- no: kjenne → kjenner
- en: to be familiar with → know/knows
:::

::: examples
- no: kunne → kan
- en: to know how to do something → can/know how to
:::

The important question is not simply whether English uses *know*. Ask what kind of knowing is involved.

## Vite: knowing a fact {#vite-knowing-a-fact role=model objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

Use **vite** when knowledge can be understood as information: a time, an address, a reason, an answer, or some other fact.

In the dialogue, Arne asks:

::: examples
- no: Vet du hvor kjøkkenet er?
- en: Do you know where the kitchen is?
:::

The location of the kitchen is information. Lea can give that information as an answer.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet hvor kjøkkenet er.
- en: I know where the kitchen is.
:::

Other common fact patterns include **vite at** for knowing that something is true and **vite hvem, hva, hvor, når, hvorfor**, or **hvordan** for knowing a piece of information.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet at møtet begynner klokka ni.
- en: I know that the meeting starts at nine.
:::

::: examples
- no: Vet du hvem som har nøkkelen?
- en: Do you know who has the key?
:::

::: examples
- no: Hun vet hvorfor bussen er forsinket.
- en: She knows why the bus is delayed.
:::

::: examples
- no: Vi vet ikke når butikken stenger.
- en: We don’t know when the shop closes.
:::

::: examples
- no: Vet dere hva adressen er?
- en: Do you know what the address is?
:::

When the fact has already been mentioned, **vite** can also be followed by a pronoun.

::: examples
- no: Vet du det?
- en: Do you know that?
:::

::: examples
- no: Ja, jeg vet det.
- en: Yes, I know that.
:::

**Checkpoint: identify information**

Read several short situations involving times, names, locations, and reasons. For each one, decide whether the speaker is asking for or reporting factual information. Then complete a sentence with the present form of **vite**.

This activity belongs here because it isolates the clearest use of the first verb before introducing close contrasts. A successful response demonstrates that the learner recognizes facts as information and chooses **vet**, even when English simply says *know*.

{{exercise: checkpoint_identify_information}}

## Kjenne: being familiar with someone or something {#kjenne-being-familiar-with-someone-or-something role=contrast objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

Use **kjenne** when the point is familiarity gained through contact or experience. You may **kjenne** a person because you have met and interacted with them. You may also **kjenne** a city, neighbourhood, building, book, or type of situation because it is familiar to you.

Arne asks Lea:

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du Mari?
- en: Do you know Mari?
:::

He is not asking whether Lea has information about Mari. He wants to know whether Mari is personally familiar to her.

Lea answers:

::: examples
- no: Nei, men jeg vet hvem hun er.
- en: No, but I know who she is.
:::

This contrast is useful:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner Mari.
- en: I know Mari.
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet hvem Mari er.
- en: I know who Mari is.
:::

The first sentence suggests personal familiarity. The second says only that the speaker knows Mari’s identity. You can know who someone is without knowing that person personally.

The same distinction appears with places.

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner Trondheim ganske godt.
- en: I know Trondheim quite well.
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet hvor Trondheim ligger.
- en: I know where Trondheim is.
:::

In the first sentence, the speaker is familiar with the city. Perhaps they have lived there, visited often, or know their way around. In the second, the speaker knows a geographical fact.

More examples:

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du den nye naboen?
- en: Do you know the new neighbour?
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner ikke dette området så godt.
- en: I don’t know this area very well.
:::

::: examples
- no: Hun kjenner restauranten fra før.
- en: She is already familiar with the restaurant.
:::

::: examples
- no: Vi kjenner familien hennes godt.
- en: We know her family well.
:::

::: examples
- no: Han kjenner systemet etter mange år i firmaet.
- en: He knows the system after many years at the company.
:::

The phrase **kjenne til** often means to be familiar with something or to know of its existence.

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du til denne forfatteren?
- en: Are you familiar with this author?
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner til problemet, men jeg vet ikke alle detaljene.
- en: I am aware of the problem, but I don’t know all the details.
:::

The contrast remains the same: **kjenne til problemet** expresses familiarity or awareness, while **vite hvorfor problemet oppstod** expresses factual knowledge.

**Checkpoint: familiarity or identity**

Return to Lea’s first day at the community centre. Imagine she has seen Mari’s name on the schedule but has never spoken to her. Describe what Lea knows using one sentence about Mari’s identity and one sentence about personal familiarity.

This activity belongs here because the same person can be the subject of two different kinds of knowledge. A successful response demonstrates the difference between **vite hvem noen er** and **kjenne noen**, rather than treating the verbs as interchangeable.

{{exercise: checkpoint_familiarity_or_identity}}

## Kunne: having a learned skill or ability {#kunne-having-a-learned-skill-or-ability role=model objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

Use **kunne** when someone has learned how to do something or has the ability to do it. Its present form is **kan**.

At the community centre, Arne asks:

::: examples
- no: Kan du lage kaffe med den store maskinen?
- en: Do you know how to make coffee with the large machine?
:::

Lea has used this type of machine before, so she answers:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan bruke en slik maskin.
- en: I know how to use that kind of machine.
:::

With a verb, **kan** is normally followed by the infinitive without **å**.

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan svømme.
- en: I can swim.
:::

::: examples
- no: Hun kan reparere sykler.
- en: She knows how to repair bicycles.
:::

::: examples
- no: Kan dere bruke dette programmet?
- en: Do you know how to use this program?
:::

::: examples
- no: Han kan ikke kjøre bil.
- en: He cannot drive.
:::

A language can also be treated as a learned ability.

::: examples
- no: Kan du norsk?
- en: Do you know Norwegian?
:::

::: examples
- no: Hun kan litt spansk.
- en: She knows a little Spanish.
:::

Keep the focus on an ability the person possesses. Compare factual knowledge with practical ability:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet hvordan kaffemaskinen fungerer.
- en: I know how the coffee machine works.
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan bruke kaffemaskinen.
- en: I know how to use the coffee machine.
:::

The first sentence concerns information about the machine’s operation. The second says that the speaker has the practical ability to operate it. Someone might understand how a machine works in theory but still be unable to use it confidently.

Another contrast:

::: examples
- no: Emil vet hvordan man skifter et dekk.
- en: Emil knows how to change a tyre.
:::

::: examples
- no: Emil kan skifte et dekk.
- en: Emil knows how to change a tyre.
:::

The sentence with **vet hvordan** reports knowledge of the procedure. The sentence with **kan** presents changing a tyre as Emil’s skill.

**Checkpoint: information or ability**

Use the event context again. Lea has read and understood the operating procedure for the sound system, but she has never operated it. Describe her factual knowledge of how the system works, then state whether the available context supports saying that she has the practical skill to use it.

This activity belongs here because it prevents a common overgeneralisation: seeing English *know how* and automatically choosing one Norwegian verb. A successful response uses **vite hvordan** for Lea’s established knowledge of the procedure and does not use **kunne** to claim an operating ability that the context has not established.

{{exercise: checkpoint_information_or_ability}}

## Three questions with three meanings {#three-questions-with-three-meanings role=contrast objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

The difference becomes clearer when all three verbs appear in similar questions.

::: examples
- no: Vet du hvem pianisten er?
- en: Do you know who the pianist is?
:::

This asks for a fact: the pianist’s identity.

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du pianisten?
- en: Do you know the pianist?
:::

This asks about personal familiarity.

::: examples
- no: Kan du spille piano?
- en: Can you play the piano?
:::

This asks about a learned skill.

Here is another aligned set:

::: examples
- no: Vet du hvor svømmehallen er?
- en: Do you know where the swimming pool is?
:::

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du svømmehallen?
- en: Are you familiar with the swimming pool?
:::

::: examples
- no: Kan du svømme?
- en: Can you swim?
:::

The first question asks for location information. The second asks whether the place is familiar. The third asks about an ability.

And one more:

::: examples
- no: Vet du hva dette programmet heter?
- en: Do you know what this program is called?
:::

::: examples
- no: Kjenner du dette programmet?
- en: Are you familiar with this program?
:::

::: examples
- no: Kan du bruke dette programmet?
- en: Do you know how to use this program?
:::

These questions could all arise in one conversation, but they request different kinds of knowledge.

**Checkpoint: compare matched situations**

For each of several shared topics—a colleague, a town, a computer program, and a musical instrument—form or complete questions that ask separately about a fact, familiarity, and a learned ability. Keep each topic constant while changing the kind of knowing.

This activity belongs here because aligned contrasts make the meaning of the verb visible without changing the surrounding context too much. A successful response demonstrates consistent choice of **vet**, **kjenner**, and **kan** according to meaning rather than according to the English word *know*.

{{exercise: checkpoint_compare_matched_situations}}

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes role=contrast objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

**Using vite for a person you know**

Avoid using **vite** directly with a person when you mean personal familiarity.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg vet Mari.
- en: I know Mari.
:::

Natural:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner Mari.
- en: I know Mari.
:::

Use **vite** when what follows is information about Mari.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet at Mari jobber her.
- en: I know that Mari works here.
:::

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet hvem Mari er.
- en: I know who Mari is.
:::

**Using kjenne for a fact**

A time, reason, answer, or location is normally information, so use **vite**.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Jeg kjenner når toget går.
- en: I know when the train leaves.
:::

Natural:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet når toget går.
- en: I know when the train leaves.
:::

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Kjenner du hvor nøkkelen er?
- en: Do you know where the key is?
:::

Natural:

::: examples
- no: Vet du hvor nøkkelen er?
- en: Do you know where the key is?
:::

**Stating a learned skill neutrally**

For an ordinary neutral statement that someone has learned and can perform an action, normally use **kunne**. Constructions with **vite å**, such as the following, can occur in marked or idiomatic contexts, but they are not the usual neutral choices for the meanings intended here.

Marked or context-dependent:

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet å svømme.
- en: I know how to swim.
:::

Usual neutral wording:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan svømme.
- en: I can swim.
:::

Marked or context-dependent:

::: examples
- no: Hun vet å spille gitar.
- en: She knows how to play guitar.
:::

Usual neutral wording:

::: examples
- no: Hun kan spille gitar.
- en: She can play the guitar.
:::

**Assuming familiarity always includes detailed facts**

Being familiar with something does not mean knowing every fact about it.

::: examples
- no: Jeg kjenner byen, men jeg vet ikke hvor det nye biblioteket er.
- en: I know the city, but I don’t know where the new library is.
:::

Likewise, factual knowledge does not necessarily mean personal familiarity.

::: examples
- no: Jeg vet mye om forfatteren, men jeg kjenner henne ikke.
- en: I know a lot about the author, but I don’t know her personally.
:::

## Back at the community centre {#back-at-the-community-centre role=model objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

Later that morning, after Mari, Lea, and Arne have spent time setting up rooms and talking together, Mari and Lea go into the kitchen to prepare coffee.

::: {.reading translation='Hi, Lea. We’ve been working together for a while now. Did you know Arne before today?' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='mari' speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Hei, Lea. Nå har vi jobbet sammen en stund. Kjenner du Arne fra før?
:::

::: {.reading translation='No, I met him today. But after working together, I know both you and Arne a little now.' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Nei, jeg møtte ham i dag. Men etter at vi har jobbet sammen, kjenner jeg både deg og Arne litt nå.
:::

::: {.reading translation='Do you know where we should put the cups?' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='mari' speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Vet du hvor vi skal sette koppene?
:::

::: {.reading translation='Yes, Arne showed me the cupboard.' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Ja, Arne viste meg skapet.
:::

::: {.reading translation='Good. Do you also know how to make tea with this machine?' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='mari' speaker_name="Mari" character_id="mari"}
Bra. Kan du også lage te med denne maskinen?
:::

::: {.reading translation='I can do that. Now I know the kitchen quite well too.' dialogue_id='later-morning-community-centre' speaker_id='lea' speaker_name="Lea" character_id="lea"}
Det kan jeg. Nå kjenner jeg kjøkkenet ganske godt også.
:::

Lea’s knowledge has changed during the morning. She now **kjenner** Mari a little because they have spent time talking and working together. She **vet** where the cups belong because Arne gave her that information. She **kan** make tea because she has the practical ability to use the machine.

**Contextual practice: Lea explains her first day**

Imagine that Lea sends a message to a friend after the event. Ask the learner to describe what Lea now knows about the building, the organisers, the schedule, and the kitchen tasks. The response should include facts, familiarity, and skills, but every sentence should still serve the single goal of choosing among **vite**, **kjenne**, and **kunne**.

This activity belongs here because it returns to the chapter’s people and situation while requiring the learner to choose independently among all three verbs. A successful response demonstrates that the learner can follow changes in context: spending time talking and working with a person can create personal familiarity, receiving information creates factual knowledge, and having learned a task supports a statement of ability.

## Further practice in new situations {#further-practice-in-new-situations role=recap objectives=obj-knowing-verbs-vite-kjenne-kunne-01}

**At a new workplace**

Picture a new employee who has read the staff list, has worked closely with one colleague throughout the first week, and has previous experience with the company’s accounting software. Complete short statements about the colleague’s name, personal familiarity with that colleague and the rest of the team, and ability to use the software.

The context gives distinct evidence for each verb. A successful response uses **vite** for information from the staff list, **kjenne** for the colleague the employee has worked closely with without claiming familiarity with the rest of the team, and **kunne** for the learned software skill.

{{exercise: further_practice_new_workplace}}

**Visiting another town**

Picture two friends arriving in Bergen. One knows the address of their hotel, while the other has visited the city many times and can drive confidently in heavy traffic. Form questions and statements about the hotel’s location, familiarity with Bergen, and driving ability.

This activity transfers the same contrast from people and workplace equipment to travel. A successful response keeps factual location, familiarity with a place, and learned ability separate.

{{exercise: further_practice_visiting_another_town}}

**Planning a concert**

Picture an organiser who knows the starting time, is personally acquainted with the singer, and knows how to operate the sound equipment. Write a brief exchange in which another organiser asks about all three kinds of knowledge.

This practice increases independence while remaining tightly focused. A successful exchange uses **vet** for the concert time, **kjenner** for the singer, and **kan** for operating the equipment.

{{exercise: further_practice_planning_a_concert}}
