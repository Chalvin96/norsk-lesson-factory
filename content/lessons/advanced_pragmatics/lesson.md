## A planning conversation {#planning-conversation role=orient objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

Maja and Henrik are organizing a summer gathering in the courtyard of their apartment building. They need to keep costs down and avoid disturbing the neighbors.

::: {.reading translation="Should we order food for everyone?" dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Skal vi bestille mat til alle?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Won’t that be a little expensive?" dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Det blir vel litt dyrt?
:::

::: {.reading translation="True, but we don’t have to order everything, as you know. People can bring something themselves." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Jo, men vi trenger jo ikke bestille alt. Folk kan ta med noe selv.
:::

::: {.reading translation="We can probably manage with a few salads and some bread." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Vi kan nok klare oss med noen salater og litt brød.
:::

::: {.reading translation="What I had actually been thinking was that we could have a barbecue." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Egentlig hadde jeg tenkt at vi kunne grille.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That’s a good idea, of course. Then we won’t have to plan the whole menu." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Det er jo en god idé. Da slipper vi å planlegge hele menyen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="So, we buy the essentials, and people bring the rest?" dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Altså, vi kjøper inn det viktigste, og så tar folk med resten?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, that sounds fine. By the way, have you spoken to Ingrid on the ground floor?" dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Ja, det høres greit ut. Forresten, har du snakket med Ingrid i første etasje?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Not yet. She’s probably still annoyed after last year’s party." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Ikke ennå. Hun er vel fortsatt irritert etter festen i fjor.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I can speak to her, then." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_opening" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Jeg kan snakke med henne, da.
:::

The basic facts in this conversation are simple. Maja suggests ordering food and later proposes a barbecue. Henrik worries about the cost, and they agree on a compromise. Near the end, he brings up a potentially difficult neighbor.

What makes the exchange sound natural is not only the information. Small words such as **vel**, **jo**, **nok**, **egentlig**, **altså**, **forresten**, and **da** show how each speaker relates to that information. They can make a statement tentative, present something as shared knowledge, indicate probability, adjust a proposal, draw a conclusion, or move to a new topic.

These are often called pragmatic markers. Their meaning depends heavily on context and tone. They rarely have one fixed English translation.

## Hearing stance rather than facts {#hearing-stance-rather-than-facts role=contrast objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

Compare these two questions:

::: examples
- no: Det blir litt dyrt?
- en: It will be a little expensive?
- no: Det blir vel litt dyrt?
- en: Won’t that be a little expensive?
:::

The first version can sound abrupt or surprised. With **vel**, Henrik presents the expense as his expectation and invites Maja to confirm or respond. He is not stating with complete certainty that the plan is too expensive.

Now compare Maja’s proposal:

::: examples
- no: Jeg hadde tenkt at vi kunne grille.
- en: I had thought that we could have a barbecue.
- no: Egentlig hadde jeg tenkt at vi kunne grille.
- en: What I had actually been thinking was that we could have a barbecue.
:::

**Egentlig** signals that Maja is revealing or returning to her underlying idea. It gently adjusts the direction of the conversation without directly rejecting Henrik’s suggestion.

**Checkpoint: noticing-changes-in-stance**

Read the opening conversation again and identify what each marker contributes. Focus on whether the speaker sounds certain, tentative, corrective, reassuring, evaluative, or ready to change topic. This belongs here because the same factual sentence can perform a different social action when a marker is added.

A successful response would explain the speakers’ intentions in context rather than provide isolated dictionary translations.

## Softening and testing an assumption with *vel* {#softening-and-testing-vel role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

**Vel** often marks an assumption as tentative or invites agreement. It is especially common when the speaker thinks something is likely but leaves room for the other person to correct it.

::: examples
- no: Du kommer vel på møtet i morgen?
- en: You’re coming to the meeting tomorrow, aren’t you?
- no: Hun har vel fått beskjed?
- en: She has probably been informed, hasn’t she?
- no: Vi bør vel begynne snart.
- en: We should probably start soon.
:::

In the first example, the speaker expects the person to attend. The question is less neutral than a simple request for information:

::: examples
- no: Kommer du på møtet i morgen?
- en: Are you coming to the meeting tomorrow?
:::

The neutral question asks for information. The version with **vel** includes an expectation.

That expectation can make a request or criticism softer:

::: examples
- no: Du kan sende rapporten i dag.
- en: You can send the report today.
- no: Du kan vel sende rapporten i dag?
- en: You can send the report today, can’t you?
:::

The second version is less openly commanding, but it still places some pressure on the listener. **Vel** is therefore not a universal politeness word. If the speaker clearly expects compliance, it may sound gently insistent.

In statements, **vel** can limit commitment:

::: examples
- no: Det er vel den beste løsningen.
- en: It is probably the best solution.
:::

The speaker offers an evaluation while acknowledging possible uncertainty or disagreement.

**Controlled practice: make-assumptions-less-absolute**

Take several direct statements about Maja and Henrik’s event and reformulate them as tentative expectations with **vel**. Use contexts such as the cost of food, the weather, the number of guests, and Ingrid’s reaction.

This activity belongs here because it isolates the difference between a firm claim and an assumption offered for confirmation. A successful response would place **vel** naturally and preserve the original situation while making the speaker’s commitment less absolute.

{{exercise: make_assumptions_less_absolute}}

## Revealing an underlying thought with *egentlig* {#revealing-underlying-thought-egentlig role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

The central meaning of **egentlig** is connected with what is underlying, original, or true beneath the current appearance. Depending on context, English may use actually, really, originally, or a longer phrase.

In the dialogue, Maja says:

::: examples
- no: Egentlig hadde jeg tenkt at vi kunne grille.
- en: What I had actually been thinking was that we could have a barbecue.
:::

She does not announce that Henrik is wrong. Instead, she gently introduces the idea she had before the discussion moved toward salads and bread.

**Egentlig** is useful when adjusting a plan:

::: examples
- no: Jeg skulle egentlig jobbe hjemme, men jeg dro inn på kontoret.
- en: I was originally going to work from home, but I went into the office.
- no: Vi hadde egentlig tenkt å reise på fredag.
- en: We had actually planned to leave on Friday.
:::

It can also soften a potentially personal question by presenting it as something the speaker has been wondering:

::: examples
- no: Hva mener du egentlig?
- en: What do you actually mean?
- no: Hvorfor sluttet du egentlig?
- en: Why did you leave, really?
:::

Tone matters. A calm question can show genuine curiosity. Strong stress on **egentlig** may sound challenging, as if the listener has been unclear or evasive.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Vil du være med?
- en: Do you want to come along?
- no: Har du egentlig lyst til å være med?
- en: Do you actually want to come along?
:::

The second question looks beneath a polite or expected answer and asks about the person’s real preference.

**Checkpoint: adjust-rather-than-reject**

Imagine that Henrik has proposed holding the gathering indoors. Respond as Maja and use **egentlig** to introduce her original preference for the courtyard. Then create a similar response in a workplace or travel context.

This activity belongs here because **egentlig** often helps speakers redirect a discussion without a blunt contradiction. A successful response would reveal an underlying intention or preference while remaining connected to what was said before.

## Presenting information as shared with *jo* {#presenting-information-as-shared-jo role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

Unstressed **jo** often signals that the information should already be known, accepted, or obvious in the conversation.

Maja says:

::: examples
- no: Vi trenger jo ikke bestille alt.
- en: We don’t have to order everything, as you know.
:::

She presents this as a reason Henrik can readily accept. Later, Henrik reacts to the barbecue proposal:

::: examples
- no: Det er jo en god idé.
- en: That’s a good idea, of course.
:::

Here **jo** creates a sense of recognition: once Maja mentions the idea, its advantage seems evident.

Other common examples include:

::: examples
- no: Du kjenner jo Ingrid.
- en: You know Ingrid, after all.
- no: Det er jo søndag i morgen.
- en: It’s Sunday tomorrow, you know.
- no: Vi kan jo spørre de andre.
- en: We could always ask the others.
:::

In the final example, **jo** presents the suggestion as an available and fairly obvious solution. English may use simply, after all, of course, as you know, or no explicit equivalent.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Vi kan spørre de andre.
- en: We can ask the others.
- no: Vi kan jo spørre de andre.
- en: We could always ask the others.
:::

The first sentence gives information about an option. The second points to that option as something readily available to both speakers.

Be careful with **jo** when disagreement is possible:

::: examples
- no: Dette har jeg jo forklart før.
- en: I have explained this before, as you know.
:::

This can sound impatient or patronizing because the speaker treats the information as already established. **Jo** creates solidarity when knowledge is genuinely shared, but friction when the listener does not accept the speaker’s assumption.

At the beginning of Maja’s reply, **Jo, men …** also responds to Henrik’s negative implication:

::: examples
- no: Jo, men vi trenger jo ikke bestille alt.
- en: True, but we don’t have to order everything, as you know.
:::

The first **jo** acknowledges or concedes Henrik’s concern. **Men** introduces the qualification that follows. The second **jo** marks the following reason as accessible shared knowledge.

**Controlled practice: supply-a-shared-reason**

Respond to concerns about the courtyard event by adding a reason with **jo**. The concern might involve rain, noise, cost, or cleanup. Keep the reason something both speakers could reasonably know.

This activity belongs here because **jo** works best when its implied shared knowledge is believable. A successful response would not merely insert **jo** mechanically; it would use the marker to present a relevant fact or solution as already available to both speakers.

## Evaluating likelihood with *nok* {#evaluating-likelihood-nok role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

When **nok** comments on how likely something is, it usually means probably or I expect. It allows a speaker to make a prediction without claiming certainty.

::: examples
- no: Vi kan nok klare oss med noen salater.
- en: We can probably manage with a few salads.
- no: Det går nok bra.
- en: It will probably be fine.
- no: Hun kommer nok litt senere.
- en: She will probably arrive a little later.
- no: De har nok glemt avtalen.
- en: They have probably forgotten the appointment.
:::

This use of **nok** often follows the finite verb:

::: examples
- no: Hun blir nok med.
- en: She will probably come along.
:::

It is different from **nok** meaning enough:

::: examples
- no: Vi har nok stoler.
- en: We have enough chairs.
:::

In the first kind of sentence, **nok** evaluates probability. In the second, it describes sufficient quantity.

Compare the stance of **vel** and **nok**:

::: examples
- no: Hun kommer vel senere?
- en: She’ll probably come later, won’t she?
- no: Hun kommer nok senere.
- en: She’ll probably come later.
:::

With **vel**, the speaker tends to invite confirmation. With **nok**, the speaker gives an assessment or prediction. Both can express uncertainty, but they organize the conversation differently.

**Checkpoint: prediction-or-confirmation**

Create paired sentences about the gathering. In one sentence, use **vel** to check an expectation with another person. In the other, use **nok** to state your own likely assessment.

This activity belongs here because the two markers can receive the same English translation even though they have different conversational functions. A successful response would show an expectation seeking confirmation with **vel** and a probability judgment with **nok**.

## Drawing a conclusion with *altså* {#drawing-a-conclusion-also role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

**Altså** can introduce a conclusion, summary, reformulation, or clarification. In the dialogue, Maja checks the practical result of their discussion:

::: examples
- no: Altså, vi kjøper inn det viktigste, og så tar folk med resten?
- en: So, we buy the essentials, and people bring the rest?
:::

She is gathering the previous suggestions into one conclusion.

::: examples
- no: Ingrid kan ikke på fredag. Altså må vi finne en annen dag.
- en: Ingrid can’t make it on Friday. So we have to find another day.
- no: Møtet begynner klokka ni, altså en time tidligere enn vanlig.
- en: The meeting starts at nine, that is, one hour earlier than usual.
- no: Du mener altså at vi bør avlyse?
- en: So you mean that we should cancel?
:::

The last example checks the implication of what another person has said. Depending on tone, it can be neutral, surprised, or skeptical.

Do not confuse Norwegian **altså** with English also. English also usually corresponds to **også**.

::: examples
- no: Ingrid kommer også.
- en: Ingrid is coming too.
- no: Ingrid kommer altså.
- en: So Ingrid is coming.
:::

The difference is crucial. **Også** adds Ingrid to a group. **Altså** presents her attendance as a conclusion or clarified fact.

In emotional speech, **altså** can also strengthen a reaction:

::: examples
- no: Det var utrolig hyggelig, altså.
- en: That was incredibly nice, honestly.
:::

This use evaluates the situation strongly, but it remains related to the same pragmatic function: the speaker highlights how the statement should be understood.

**Controlled practice: summarize-an-agreement**

Summarize Maja and Henrik’s arrangement with **altså**. Then use the marker to check the implication of a change, such as moving the event indoors or asking guests to bring food.

This activity belongs here because **altså** often appears when speakers turn several details into one practical conclusion. A successful response would make the conclusion clear and logically connected to the preceding context.

{{exercise: summarize_agreement}}

## Shifting topic with *forresten* {#shifting-topic-forresten role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

**Forresten** introduces a new thought that is not the main continuation of the current topic. English often uses by the way.

::: examples
- no: Forresten, har du snakket med Ingrid?
- en: By the way, have you spoken to Ingrid?
- no: Jeg sender deg planen senere. Forresten, hva er e-postadressen din?
- en: I’ll send you the plan later. By the way, what is your email address?
- no: Forresten, takk for hjelpen i går.
- en: By the way, thanks for your help yesterday.
:::

The new topic should normally have some plausible connection to the situation or to something the speaker has just remembered. **Forresten** is useful precisely because it signals that the transition is not completely smooth.

It is less suitable when the new information is an urgent correction:

::: examples
- no: Vent, møtet er avlyst.
- en: Wait, the meeting has been canceled.
:::

Using **forresten** here could make an important announcement sound oddly casual.

**Checkpoint: make-a-believable-topic-shift**

Continue a short conversation about food for the gathering, then use **forresten** to introduce a related practical matter such as music, keys, invitations, or a neighbor.

This activity belongs here because topic shifts sound natural only when the new thought fits the wider situation. A successful response would clearly mark the shift without making the added information seem random or inappropriately casual.

{{exercise: believable_topic_shift}}

## Responding to circumstances with *da* {#responding-to-circumstances-da role=model objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

**Da** can refer to time, but it also has conversational uses. At the end of the opening dialogue, Henrik says:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan snakke med henne, da.
- en: I can speak to her, then.
:::

His offer follows from Maja’s statement that she has not yet spoken to Ingrid. **Da** presents the offer as a response to the circumstances: if Maja has not done it, Henrik can.

::: examples
- no: Jeg tar bussen, da.
- en: I’ll take the bus, then.
- no: Vi får møtes på mandag, da.
- en: We’ll have to meet on Monday, then.
- no: Det er greit, da.
- en: All right, then.
:::

Depending on tone, **da** may express acceptance, mild reluctance, insistence, or a practical consequence. The last example can sound warmly accepting or slightly resigned.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Jeg kan rydde etterpå.
- en: I can clean up afterward.
- no: Jeg kan rydde etterpå, da.
- en: I can clean up afterward, then.
:::

The first is a straightforward offer. The second sounds more directly prompted by what has just been said.

Do not assume that every **da** is a pragmatic marker:

::: examples
- no: Da jeg kom hjem, hadde alle gått.
- en: When I came home, everyone had left.
:::

Here **da** introduces a time clause. Its function is grammatical rather than conversational.

**Controlled practice: respond-to-a-new-circumstance**

Write brief responses to changes in the event plan, using sentence-final **da**. Someone cannot buy the food, the courtyard is unavailable, or Maja has not contacted Ingrid. Make each response a consequence, compromise, or offer.

This activity belongs here because pragmatic **da** gains meaning from the previous turn. A successful response would react naturally to a stated circumstance rather than use **da** as an isolated filler.

## Returning to the courtyard {#returning-to-the-courtyard role=contrast objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

A few days later, Maja and Henrik speak again.

Jeg har snakket med Ingrid. Hun kommer nok på festen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That’s good! She wasn’t still angry, was she?" dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Så bra! Hun var vel ikke fortsatt sint?
:::

::: {.reading translation="No. What she was really most concerned about was the music." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Nei. Egentlig var hun mest bekymret for musikken.
:::

::: {.reading translation="I can understand that, of course. She goes to bed early." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Det skjønner jeg jo. Hun legger seg tidlig.
:::

::: {.reading translation="So, if we turn down the music at eleven, she’ll be satisfied." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Altså, hvis vi skrur ned musikken klokka elleve, er hun fornøyd.
:::

::: {.reading translation="That will probably be fine. We can tell everyone in advance, after all." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Det går nok fint. Vi kan jo si fra til alle på forhånd.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes. By the way, the weather forecast looks bad." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="henrik" speaker_name="Henrik" character_id="henrik"}
Ja. Forresten, værmeldingen ser dårlig ut.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then we’ll move the long table inside if it starts raining." dialogue_id="planning_conversation_return" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}
Da flytter vi langbordet inn hvis det begynner å regne.
:::

The markers now perform the same functions in a new stage of the situation:

- **nok** evaluates the likelihood that Ingrid will attend and that the plan will work.
- **vel** checks Maja’s hopeful assumption.
- **egentlig** identifies Ingrid’s underlying concern.
- **jo** presents relevant information as shared or readily understandable.
- **altså** states the practical conclusion of Henrik’s conversation with Ingrid.
- **forresten** shifts from the neighbor to the weather.
- **da** introduces the response that follows if it rains.

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes role=recap objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

Avoid translating each marker mechanically. In many English translations, no separate word is necessary.

Do not use **jo** to mark information as shared when the listener could not reasonably know it. That can sound dismissive.

Do not treat **vel** as automatically polite. It expresses an expectation and may place pressure on the listener.

Distinguish probability **nok** from quantity **nok**:

::: examples
- no: Vi rekker nok bussen.
- en: We will probably catch the bus.
- no: Vi har nok tid.
- en: We have enough time.
:::

Keep **altså** and **også** separate:

::: examples
- no: Vi inviterer altså Ingrid.
- en: So we are inviting Ingrid.
- no: Vi inviterer også Ingrid.
- en: We are inviting Ingrid too.
:::

Use **forresten** for a genuine side topic or remembered point, not for information that must be presented as an urgent warning.

Remember that sentence-final **da** needs a relationship to the preceding context. Without that relationship, it may sound unnecessary or unclear.

**Repair practice: choose-a-marker-that-fits**

Before attempting this repair, make sure you can distinguish **forresten**, which marks a side topic or remembered point, from **da**, which can present a response to preceding circumstances.

Henrik says that Ingrid is worried about the music. Maja replies:

::: examples
- no: Forresten, vi skrur ned musikken klokka elleve.
- en: By the way, we’ll turn down the music at eleven.
:::

Revise Maja’s turn while preserving the intended message that lowering the music is a response to Ingrid’s concern. Explain how your correction changes the interpersonal effect. Then use the corrected function once in a meeting or travel context where a speaker responds to a newly stated circumstance.

## Integrated practice: manage-a-planning-conversation {#integrated-practice role=recap objectives=obj-advanced-pragmatics-01}

Continue the courtyard discussion after the weather forecast changes. Include a tentative expectation, an underlying preference, a shared reason, a probability judgment, a practical conclusion, a topic shift, and a response to new circumstances. Use the markers from this chapter where they fit naturally rather than trying to place one in every sentence.

This activity belongs near the end because the markers now need to work together across several turns. A successful response would keep the event planning coherent while showing shifts in certainty, stance, implication, and topic management.

Next, move the same forms to a different situation, such as colleagues rearranging a meeting or friends changing travel plans. Keep the functions stable: **vel** checks an expectation, **egentlig** reveals an underlying intention, **jo** invokes shared ground, **nok** evaluates likelihood, **altså** draws out a conclusion, **forresten** shifts topic, and **da** responds to circumstances.

A successful response would reuse the markers flexibly without copying the courtyard dialogue or forcing direct English equivalents.
