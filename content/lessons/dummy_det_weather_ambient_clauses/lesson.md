## A change of plans {#a-change-of-plans role=orient objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Mina and Jonas are preparing to meet outside a café in Bergen. Mina is already on her way, but the weather is getting worse.

::: {.reading translation="Hi, Jonas. Are you on your way?" dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Hei, Jonas. Er du på vei?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Not yet. What is the weather like there?" dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ikke ennå. Hvordan er været der?
:::

::: {.reading translation="It’s raining, and it’s quite windy." dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Det regner, og det blåser ganske mye.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Oh no. Is it cold too?" dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Å nei. Er det kaldt også?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, it’s cold, and it’s starting to get dark." dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ja, det er kaldt, og det begynner å bli mørkt.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then we’ll meet inside the café." dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Da møtes vi inne på kafeen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good idea. It’s warm and pleasant inside." dialogue_id=cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
God idé. Det er varmt og hyggelig der inne.
:::

Mina uses *det* several times because she is describing weather and general conditions around her:

::: examples
- no: Det regner.
- en: It’s raining.
- no: Det blåser.
- en: It’s windy. / The wind is blowing.
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
- no: Det blir mørkt.
- en: It’s getting dark.
- no: Det er varmt der inne.
- en: It’s warm inside.
:::

In these sentences, *det* does not identify a particular object. Mina is not saying that some object called “it” is raining, cold, or dark. Norwegian requires a grammatical subject in these statements, so it uses fixed *det* when weather or an ambient condition has no real person or thing performing the action or possessing the quality.

## Saying what conditions are like {#saying-what-conditions-are-like role=model objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

English also commonly uses “it” in weather expressions:

- *It is raining.*
- *It is cold.*
- *It is dark.*

Norwegian uses *det* in much the same way. This use is often called a dummy subject or formal subject. You do not need to analyze that label when speaking. The practical point is simple:

::: {.rule}
When you describe weather or a general condition and there is no actual person or thing to use as the subject, begin the statement with fixed *det*.
:::

The basic patterns in this chapter are:

**det + weather verb**

::: examples
- no: Det regner.
- en: It’s raining.
- no: Det snør.
- en: It’s snowing.
- no: Det blåser.
- en: It’s windy. / The wind is blowing.
:::

and:

**det + er/blir + description**

::: examples
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
- no: Det er varmt.
- en: It’s warm.
- no: Det er lyst.
- en: It’s light.
- no: Det er mørkt.
- en: It’s dark.
- no: Det blir kaldt.
- en: It’s getting cold.
- no: Det blir mørkt.
- en: It’s getting dark.
:::

The important choice is not whether English happens to include the word “it” in every translation. The important choice is whether Norwegian needs a subject even though no particular person or thing is being described.

## Weather verbs with det {#weather-verbs-with-det role=model objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Some common Norwegian weather descriptions use a verb directly after *det*.

::: examples
- no: Det regner.
- en: It’s raining.
- no: Det snør.
- en: It’s snowing.
- no: Det blåser.
- en: It’s windy. / The wind is blowing.
:::

These are complete sentences. Do not add *er* before the weather verb.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Det regner.
- en: It’s raining.
- no: Det er regn.
- en: There is rain.
:::

The first is the ordinary way to report what the weather is doing. The second is possible in limited contexts, but it does not replace the everyday weather expression **Det regner**.

The same applies to snow:

::: examples
- no: Det snør i Oslo.
- en: It’s snowing in Oslo.
- no: Det snør mye i dag.
- en: It’s snowing a lot today.
- no: Det blåser ved sjøen.
- en: It’s windy by the sea.
:::

A likely mistake is to omit *det* because the learner thinks the weather verb already gives enough information:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Regner i dag.
- en: Incorrect: Raining today.
- no: Det regner i dag.
- en: It’s raining today.
:::

Norwegian normally requires the grammatical subject *det* here.

Another likely mistake is to translate English “It is raining” word for word by inserting *er*:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Det er regner.
- en: Incorrect: It is raining.
- no: Det regner.
- en: It’s raining.
:::

The compact rule is:

::: {.rule}
Use **det + regner/snør/blåser**. These weather verbs do not need *er*.
:::

## Describing cold, warmth, light, and darkness {#describing-ambient-conditions role=model objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Other conditions use *det* with **er** and a descriptive word:

::: examples
- no: Det er kaldt ute.
- en: It’s cold outside.
- no: Det er varmt på kjøkkenet.
- en: It’s warm in the kitchen.
- no: Det er mørkt nå.
- en: It’s dark now.
- no: Det er lyst om morgenen.
- en: It’s light in the morning.
:::

These sentences describe the general surroundings. There is no particular object that is cold, warm, dark, or light.

## Named subjects and ambient det {#named-subject-and-ambient-det role=contrast objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Compare the following two uses:

::: examples
- no: Kaffen er kald.
- en: The coffee is cold.
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
:::

In the first sentence, **kaffen** is the actual subject. The sentence describes the coffee. In the second, there is no named thing being described. The sentence reports the general temperature, so Norwegian uses fixed *det*.

The same contrast appears here:

::: examples
- no: Rommet er mørkt.
- en: The room is dark.
- no: Det er mørkt.
- en: It’s dark.
:::

**Rommet er mørkt** describes a specific room. **Det er mørkt** describes the ambient condition without naming a specific subject.

And here:

::: examples
- no: Ovnen er varm.
- en: The oven is hot.
- no: Det er varmt.
- en: It’s warm.
:::

The descriptive form differs because a named noun may have its own grammatical form:

::: examples
- no: Kaffen er kald.
- en: The coffee is cold.
- no: Ovnen er varm.
- en: The oven is hot.
- no: Rommet er mørkt.
- en: The room is dark.
:::

With fixed *det* in these ambient expressions, use:

::: examples
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
- no: Det er varmt.
- en: It’s warm.
- no: Det er mørkt.
- en: It’s dark.
:::

At this level, it is most useful to learn these as stable expressions. A common mistake is to copy the form used with a specific noun:

::: examples
- no: ✗ Det er kald.
- en: Incorrect for general temperature: It is cold.
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
- no: ✗ Det er varm.
- en: Incorrect for general warmth: It is warm.
- no: Det er varmt.
- en: It’s warm.
:::

The compact rule is:

::: {.rule}
Use **det er + kaldt/varmt/lyst/mørkt** for a general condition with no named subject.
:::

## Reporting a change with blir {#reporting-a-change-with-blir role=contrast objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Mina says:

::: examples
- no: Det begynner å bli mørkt.
- en: It’s starting to get dark.
:::

A simpler way to report the same developing condition is:

::: examples
- no: Det blir mørkt.
- en: It’s getting dark.
:::

Here, **blir** reports a change. Compare:

::: examples
- no: Det er mørkt.
- en: It’s dark.
- no: Det blir mørkt.
- en: It’s getting dark.
:::

Both sentences use fixed *det*. The difference is that **er** reports the current condition, while **blir** reports that the condition is changing.

More examples:

::: examples
- no: Det er kaldt nå.
- en: It’s cold now.
- no: Det blir kaldt i kveld.
- en: It’s getting cold this evening. / It will get cold this evening.
- no: Det er lyst ute.
- en: It’s light outside.
- no: Det blir mørkt snart.
- en: It’s getting dark soon.
:::

The target remains fixed *det*. You are using the same subject because the changing condition still has no particular person or thing as its semantic subject.

Do not replace *det* with a weather noun unless you actually want to make that noun the subject. For example:

- **Været blir kaldt** is not the ordinary way to say *It’s getting cold*.
- **Det blir kaldt** is the natural ambient expression.

## Adding place and time {#adding-place-and-time role=model objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

Fixed *det* remains in the sentence when you add information about where or when the condition applies.

::: examples
- no: Det regner i Bergen.
- en: It’s raining in Bergen.
- no: Det snør på fjellet.
- en: It’s snowing in the mountains.
- no: Det blåser mye ved sjøen.
- en: It’s very windy by the sea.
- no: Det er kaldt om morgenen.
- en: It’s cold in the morning.
- no: Det er varmt inne.
- en: It’s warm inside.
- no: Det blir mørkt tidlig om vinteren.
- en: It gets dark early in winter.
:::

The place or time expression does not become the subject. In **Det regner i Bergen**, Bergen is the location of the rain, not the grammatical subject of **regner**.

This allows short, useful exchanges:

::: {.reading translation="What is the weather like in Trondheim?" dialogue_id=local-weather speaker_id=a speaker_name=A}
Hvordan er været i Trondheim?
:::

::: {.reading translation="It’s raining, and it’s cold." dialogue_id=local-weather speaker_id=b speaker_name=B}
Det regner, og det er kaldt.
:::

::: {.reading translation="What is it like at the cabin?" dialogue_id=cabin-weather speaker_id=a speaker_name=A}
Hvordan er det på hytta?
:::

::: {.reading translation="It’s snowing, and it gets dark early." dialogue_id=cabin-weather speaker_id=b speaker_name=B}
Det snør, og det blir mørkt tidlig.
:::

The questions provide a setting, but the answers still need *det*. Do not answer with bare forms such as **er kaldt** or **snør** when giving a normal complete statement.

::: {.reading translation="Yes. I’m sitting inside now." dialogue_id=second-cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}

:::

::: {.reading translation="Is it still raining?" dialogue_id=second-cafe-call speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Regner det fortsatt?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, it’s still raining, and it’s windier than before." dialogue_id=second-cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Ja, det regner fortsatt, og det blåser mer enn før.
:::

::: {.reading translation="What is it like inside?" dialogue_id=second-cafe-call speaker_id=jonas speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Hvordan er det inne?
:::

::: {.reading translation="It’s warm here. Come inside when you arrive." dialogue_id=second-cafe-call speaker_id=mina speaker_name="Mina" character_id="mina"}
Det er varmt her. Kom inn når du kommer.
:::

The second conversation reuses the same fixed *det* in a natural development of the situation. Outside, **det regner** and **det blåser**. Inside, **det er varmt**.

Notice that *det* can also appear in a question:

::: examples
- no: Regner det?
- en: Is it raining?
- no: Er det kaldt?
- en: Is it cold?
:::

The same fixed subject is present, even though its position changes in the question. This chapter’s main goal remains recognizing and using *det* when there is no real weather or ambient subject.

## Common mistakes to avoid {#common-mistakes-to-avoid role=contrast objectives=obj-dummy-det-weather-ambient-clauses-01}

**Leaving the subject out**

Norwegian does not normally use a bare weather verb as a complete statement.

::: examples
- no: ✗ Snør på fjellet.
- en: Incorrect: Snowing in the mountains.
- no: Det snør på fjellet.
- en: It’s snowing in the mountains.
:::

**Adding *er* before a weather verb**

Use the weather verb directly after *det*.

::: examples
- no: ✗ Det er blåser.
- en: Incorrect: It is windy.
- no: Det blåser.
- en: It’s windy.
:::

**Using a named noun when no named thing is meant**

If you mean the general temperature, use fixed *det*.

::: examples
- no: ✗ Været er kaldt.
- en: Unnatural for the intended meaning: The weather is cold.
- no: Det er kaldt.
- en: It’s cold.
:::

**Treating *det* as a specific object**

In **Det regner**, *det* does not stand for the sky, the weather, the town, or the rain. It fills the subject position because Norwegian requires one.
