Maja and Jonas use several kinds of simple main clause (hovedsetning): ordinary statements, statements beginning with a time phrase, yes/no questions, and questions beginning with a question word. Their sentences look different, but the position of the present-tense verb follows a small set of dependable patterns.

## What this word order helps you do {#what-this-word-order-helps-you-do role=model objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

Norwegian word order allows you to change what comes first without losing track of the main verb. You can begin with the subject:

::: examples
- no: Jeg jobber her i dag.
- en: I’m working here today.
:::

You can begin with another phrase, such as a time expression:

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg her.
- en: Today I’m working here.
:::

You can ask a yes/no question:

::: examples
- no: Jobber du her i dag?
- en: Are you working here today?
:::

You can also begin a question with a question word:

::: examples
- no: Hvor jobber du?
- en: Where do you work?
:::

In statements and main-clause wh-questions, Norwegian places the present-tense verb in the second **slot**. This is commonly called the verb-second pattern. “Second” means the second sentence element, not necessarily the second individual word.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg her.
- en: Today I’m working here.
:::

The first slot is the complete phrase i dag. Although it contains two words, it functions as one sentence element. The verb jobber occupies the second slot.

A useful summary is:

- Subject-first statement: subject + verb
- Fronted statement: fronted phrase + verb + subject
- Yes/no question: verb + subject
- Wh-question: question word or phrase + verb + subject

Yes/no questions are the important exception to the literal idea of “verb second”: they begin with the verb.

## Subject-first statements {#subject-first-statements role=model objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

The most straightforward Norwegian statement begins with the subject—the person or thing doing, experiencing, or being something. The present-tense verb follows immediately.

- First slot: Jeg; Verb: jobber; Rest: her i dag.
- First slot: Sara; Verb: kommer; Rest: klokka ti.
- First slot: Studiegruppen; Verb: sitter; Rest: ved vinduet.
- First slot: Vi; Verb: begynner; Rest: nå.

::: examples
- no: Jeg jobber her i dag.
- en: I’m working here today.
- no: Sara kommer klokka ti.
- en: Sara is coming at ten o’clock.
- no: Studiegruppen sitter ved vinduet.
- en: The study group is sitting by the window.
- no: Vi begynner nå.
- en: We’re beginning now.
:::

English sometimes uses an extra form of do or be where Norwegian uses only the present-tense verb. Do not add an extra Norwegian verb merely because the English translation contains one.

::: examples
- no: De drikker kaffe.
- en: They are drinking coffee.
:::

The Norwegian present tense drikker can correspond to both drink and are drinking, depending on context. The word-order pattern remains subject + verb.

**Common mistake: separating the subject and verb**

Avoid placing a time or place expression between the opening subject and the present-tense verb.

**Incorrect:**

::: examples
- no: Jeg i dag jobber her.
- en: Intended meaning: I’m working here today.
:::

**Natural Norwegian:**

::: examples
- no: Jeg jobber her i dag.
- en: I’m working here today.
:::

If the subject comes first, the verb normally comes next.

**Practice request: build-subject-first-statements**

**Purpose:** Establish the basic subject + verb frame before changing the first element.

**Context:** Maja describes the people and activities at the café.

**Learner action:** Form complete statements from a supplied subject, present-tense verb, and short time or place detail. Keep the subject first and place the verb immediately after it.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct placement of the present-tense verb after a subject in a simple statement.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can independently produce the basic Norwegian main-clause frame rather than copying English word order mechanically.

## Beginning with a time or place phrase {#beginning-with-time-or-place-phrase role=model objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

Norwegian speakers often begin with information that sets the scene. In the dialogue, Jonas says:

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg ikke lenge.
- en: Today I’m not working for long.
:::

He also says:

::: examples
- no: Klokka ti begynner vi.
- en: At ten o’clock we begin.
:::

The first element is not the subject. It is a time phrase. The verb still takes the second slot, so the subject moves after the verb.

- First slot: I dag; Verb: jobber; Subject: jeg; Rest: her.
- First slot: Klokka ti; Verb: begynner; Subject: vi; Rest: .
- First slot: Nå; Verb: drikker; Subject: de; Rest: kaffe.
- First slot: På lørdager; Verb: møtes; Subject: gruppen; Rest: her.
- First slot: Ved vinduet; Verb: sitter; Subject: studiegruppen; Rest: .

::: examples
- no: Nå drikker de kaffe.
- en: Now they’re drinking coffee.
- no: På lørdager møtes gruppen her.
- en: On Saturdays the group meets here.
- no: Ved vinduet sitter studiegruppen.
- en: The study group is sitting by the window.
:::

This movement of the subject to a position after the verb is often called **inversion (inversjon)**. You do not need to memorize the label to use the pattern. The practical rule is more important:

::: {.rule}
When a simple statement begins with something other than the subject, put the present-tense verb next and then the subject.
:::

Compare the same message with two different openings:

**Subject first**

::: examples
- no: Vi begynner klokka ti.
- en: We begin at ten.
:::

**Time first**

::: examples
- no: Klokka ti begynner vi.
- en: At ten we begin.
:::

**Subject first**

::: examples
- no: De drikker kaffe nå.
- en: They’re drinking coffee now.
:::

**Time first**

::: examples
- no: Nå drikker de kaffe.
- en: Now they’re drinking coffee.
:::

**Subject first**

::: examples
- no: Gruppen møtes her på lørdager.
- en: The group meets here on Saturdays.
:::

**Time first**

::: examples
- no: På lørdager møtes gruppen her.
- en: On Saturdays the group meets here.
:::

Both versions can describe the same event. The first element changes what the speaker presents as the starting point.

**One phrase can contain several words**

The verb does not have to be the second written word.

::: examples
- no: Hver lørdagsmorgen møter Jonas studiegruppen.
- en: Every Saturday morning Jonas meets the study group.
:::

Hver lørdagsmorgen is one complete time phrase in the first slot. The verb møter follows that phrase.

::: examples
- no: På den lille kafeen møter Maja Jonas.
- en: At the little café Maja meets Jonas.
:::

På den lille kafeen is one place phrase. The verb møter still occupies the second sentence slot.

**Common mistake: keeping English subject–verb order after a fronted phrase**

**Incorrect:**

::: examples
- no: I dag jeg jobber her.
- en: Intended meaning: Today I’m working here.
:::

**Natural Norwegian:**

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg her.
- en: Today I’m working here.
:::

English normally allows Today I am working here. Norwegian requires the verb before the subject after the fronted time phrase.

**Practice request: notice-fronted-phrase-inversion**

**Purpose:** Help the learner see that “second position” refers to sentence elements rather than individual words.

**Context:** Short statements describe when and where the café group meets.

**Learner action:** Identify the opening phrase, the present-tense verb, and the subject in each statement. Treat a multiword time or place phrase as one element.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Accurate recognition of the verb’s second-slot position in fronted statements.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can locate the structural slots even when the opening phrase contains several words.

{{exercise: notice_fronted_phrase_inversion}}

**Practice request: reframe-statements-with-time-first**

**Purpose:** Move from noticing inversion to producing it.

**Context:** Maja updates a friend about the group’s Saturday schedule.

**Learner action:** Restate subject-first messages so that a supplied time phrase comes first. Preserve the meaning while changing the order to fronted phrase + verb + subject.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct inversion of verb and subject after a fronted time phrase.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can deliberately change the starting point of a statement without moving the verb out of its required position.

## Placing common sentence adverbs {#placing-common-sentence-adverbs role=model objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

Words such as ikke, alltid, ofte, and vanligvis are common **sentence adverbs (setningsadverb)**. They add meanings such as negation, frequency, or usual behavior.

::: examples
- no: ikke
- en: not
- no: alltid
- en: always
- no: ofte
- en: often
- no: vanligvis
- en: usually
:::

In a simple subject-first statement, the sentence adverb normally comes after the present-tense verb.

- Subject: Jeg; Verb: jobber; Sentence adverb: ikke; Rest: lenge.
- Subject: Hun; Verb: kommer; Sentence adverb: ikke; Rest: i dag.
- Subject: Jeg; Verb: har; Sentence adverb: alltid; Rest: tid.
- Subject: De; Verb: sitter; Sentence adverb: vanligvis; Rest: ved vinduet.
- Subject: Vi; Verb: møtes; Sentence adverb: ofte; Rest: på lørdager.

::: examples
- no: Hun kommer ikke i dag.
- en: She isn’t coming today.
- no: Jeg har alltid tid på lørdager.
- en: I always have time on Saturdays.
- no: De sitter vanligvis ved vinduet.
- en: They usually sit by the window.
:::

When another phrase comes first, the order is:

::: {.rule}
fronted phrase + verb + subject + sentence adverb
:::

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg ikke lenge.
- en: Today I’m not working for long.
- no: På lørdager sitter de vanligvis ved vinduet.
- en: On Saturdays they usually sit by the window.
- no: Nå har jeg ikke tid.
- en: Now I don’t have time.
:::

Compare the aligned patterns:

**Subject-first statement**

::: examples
- no: Jeg jobber ikke i dag.
- en: I’m not working today.
:::

**Fronted statement**

::: examples
- no: I dag jobber jeg ikke.
- en: Today I’m not working.
:::

**Subject-first statement**

::: examples
- no: De sitter vanligvis her.
- en: They usually sit here.
:::

**Fronted statement**

::: examples
- no: På lørdager sitter de vanligvis her.
- en: On Saturdays they usually sit here.
:::

**Subject-first statement**

::: examples
- no: Vi møtes ofte på kafeen.
- en: We often meet at the café.
:::

**Fronted statement**

::: examples
- no: Etter kurset møtes vi ofte på kafeen.
- en: After the course we often meet at the café.
:::

Notice what stays stable in the fronted version: the verb comes before the subject, but the sentence adverb follows the subject.

**Common mistake: putting ikke before the present-tense verb**

**Incorrect:**

::: examples
- no: Hun ikke kommer i dag.
- en: Intended meaning: She isn’t coming today.
:::

**Natural Norwegian:**

::: examples
- no: Hun kommer ikke i dag.
- en: She isn’t coming today.
:::

After a fronted phrase, avoid putting ikke between the verb and subject.

**Incorrect:**

::: examples
- no: I dag kommer ikke hun.
- en: Intended neutral meaning: Today she isn’t coming.
:::

**Natural neutral order:**

::: examples
- no: I dag kommer hun ikke.
- en: Today she isn’t coming.
:::

The incorrect version can sound as though hun is being strongly contrasted with someone else. For the ordinary neutral meaning, use verb + subject + ikke.

**Practice request: place-sentence-adverbs-in-statements**

**Purpose:** Add negation and frequency without disrupting main-clause order.

**Context:** Jonas explains who attends the study group and what usually happens there.

**Learner action:** Complete simple subject-first and time-first statements using a supplied sentence adverb. Place the adverb relative to the present-tense verb and subject.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct neutral placement of common sentence adverbs in simple main-clause statements.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can distinguish the two frames: subject + verb + adverb and fronted phrase + verb + subject + adverb.

{{exercise: place_sentence_adverbs_in_statements}}

## Yes/no questions begin with the verb {#yes-no-questions-begin-with-the-verb role=contrast objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

Maja wants to know whether Jonas works at the café:

::: examples
- no: Jobber du her i dag?
- en: Are you working here today?
:::

She also asks about Sara:

::: examples
- no: Kommer Sara også?
- en: Is Sara coming too?
:::

A yes/no question can be answered with ja or nei. In this question type, the present-tense verb comes first and the subject follows.

- Verb: Jobber; Subject: du; Rest: her i dag?
- Verb: Kommer; Subject: Sara; Rest: også?
- Verb: Har; Subject: du; Rest: tid?
- Verb: Sitter; Subject: gruppen; Rest: ved vinduet?
- Verb: Begynner; Subject: dere; Rest: klokka ti?

::: examples
- no: Har du tid?
- en: Do you have time?
- no: Sitter gruppen ved vinduet?
- en: Is the group sitting by the window?
- no: Begynner dere klokka ti?
- en: Do you begin at ten?
:::

Norwegian does not normally add an equivalent of English do or does to make these questions. Move the Norwegian present-tense verb to the beginning.

Compare:

**Statement**

::: examples
- no: Du jobber her.
- en: You work here.
:::

**Yes/no question**

::: examples
- no: Jobber du her?
- en: Do you work here?
:::

**Statement**

::: examples
- no: Sara kommer i dag.
- en: Sara is coming today.
:::

**Yes/no question**

::: examples
- no: Kommer Sara i dag?
- en: Is Sara coming today?
:::

**Statement**

::: examples
- no: Dere begynner klokka ti.
- en: You begin at ten.
:::

**Yes/no question**

::: examples
- no: Begynner dere klokka ti?
- en: Do you begin at ten?
:::

A sentence adverb normally follows the subject in a neutral yes/no question.

::: examples
- no: Kommer hun ikke i dag?
- en: Isn’t she coming today?
- no: Møtes dere ofte her?
- en: Do you often meet here?
- no: Sitter de vanligvis ved vinduet?
- en: Do they usually sit by the window?
:::

The central question frame is:

::: {.rule}
verb + subject + sentence adverb + rest
:::

**Common mistake: adding a Norwegian equivalent of do**

Do not try to translate every part of Do you work here? word for word. Norwegian forms the question through word order.

**Natural Norwegian:**

::: examples
- no: Jobber du her?
- en: Do you work here?
:::

The form jobber already carries the present tense. Its position tells the listener that this is a yes/no question.

**Practice request: turn-statements-into-yes-no-questions**

**Purpose:** Contrast verb-second statements with verb-first yes/no questions.

**Context:** Maja checks practical details about the café meeting.

**Learner action:** Change simple statements into yes/no questions by moving the present-tense verb before the subject. Retain any time, place, or sentence-adverb information in a natural position.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct verb-first formation of yes/no questions.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can recognize when the verb-second statement rule gives way to the verb-first question pattern.

## Wh-questions keep the verb in the second slot {#wh-questions-keep-the-verb-in-the-second-slot role=contrast objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

Some questions ask for specific information rather than a yes or no. They begin with a question word or question phrase.

Common forms in this lesson include:

::: examples
- no: hvor
- en: where
- no: når
- en: when
- no: hva
- en: what
:::

In the dialogue, Maja asks:

::: examples
- no: Hvor sitter studiegruppen?
- en: Where is the study group sitting?
- no: Når begynner dere?
- en: When do you begin?
:::

The question word occupies the first slot. The present-tense verb follows, and then comes the subject.

- Question element: Hvor; Verb: sitter; Subject: studiegruppen; Rest: ?
- Question element: Når; Verb: begynner; Subject: dere; Rest: ?
- Question element: Hvor; Verb: jobber; Subject: du; Rest: ?
- Question element: Hva; Verb: drikker; Subject: de; Rest: ?
- Question element: Når; Verb: kommer; Subject: Sara; Rest: ?

::: examples
- no: Hvor jobber du?
- en: Where do you work?
- no: Hva drikker de?
- en: What are they drinking?
- no: Når kommer Sara?
- en: When is Sara coming?
:::

A multiword question phrase still fills one first slot.

::: examples
- no: Når på lørdag begynner dere?
- en: When on Saturday do you begin?
:::

The complete question phrase når på lørdag comes before the verb begynner.

Sentence adverbs normally follow the subject:

::: examples
- no: Hvor sitter de vanligvis?
- en: Where do they usually sit?
- no: Når jobber du ikke?
- en: When are you not working?
:::

The practical frame is:

::: {.rule}
question word or phrase + verb + subject + sentence adverb + rest
:::

**Aligned contrast: three clause types**

**Clause purpose: Statement**

::: examples
- no: De møtes på lørdager.
- en: They meet on Saturdays.
:::

**Clause purpose: Yes/no question**

::: examples
- no: Møtes de på lørdager?
- en: Do they meet on Saturdays?
:::

**Clause purpose: Wh-question**

::: examples
- no: Når møtes de?
- en: When do they meet?
:::

Another set:

**Clause purpose: Statement**

::: examples
- no: Sara jobber her.
- en: Sara works here.
:::

**Clause purpose: Yes/no question**

::: examples
- no: Jobber Sara her?
- en: Does Sara work here?
:::

**Clause purpose: Wh-question**

::: examples
- no: Hvor jobber Sara?
- en: Where does Sara work?
:::

The present-tense verb does not change form. Its position changes according to the clause purpose.

**Common mistake: keeping statement order after a question word**

**Incorrect:**

::: examples
- no: Hvor du jobber?
- en: Intended meaning: Where do you work?
:::

**Natural Norwegian:**

::: examples
- no: Hvor jobber du?
- en: Where do you work?
:::

The question word comes first, the verb second, and the subject follows.

**Practice request: form-wh-questions-from-missing-information**

**Purpose:** Connect wh-question order to the information the speaker needs.

**Context:** Maja has incomplete information about the study group’s schedule, location, and activities.

**Learner action:** Form a question beginning with a supplied question word or phrase. Place the present-tense verb before the subject and ask for the missing information.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct question word + verb + subject order in simple wh-questions.

**Success demonstrates:** The learner can produce an information question from communicative need, not merely label a sentence pattern.

::: {.reading translation="When do you begin?" dialogue_id="weekday-follow-up" speaker_id="maja" speaker_name="Maja" character_id="maja"}

The follow-up reuses the same word-order choices for a new part of the situation:

- Kommer Sara senere? begins with the verb because it is a yes/no question.
- Hun kommer ikke i dag has subject + verb + sentence adverb.
- På søndag møter hun oss has fronted phrase + verb + subject.
- Hvor møtes dere på søndag? has question word + verb + subject.
- Vi møtes vanligvis her has subject + verb + sentence adverb.
- På søndag møtes vi på biblioteket has fronted phrase + verb + subject.
- Klokka elleve begynner vi has a multiword time phrase in the first slot, followed by the verb.

## A compact working rule {#a-compact-working-rule role=recap objectives=obj-word-order-main-clauses-01}

For simple Norwegian main clauses in the present tense, choose the frame that matches your purpose:

**Ordinary statement**

::: {.rule}
subject + verb + sentence adverb + rest
:::

::: examples
- no: Vi møtes ofte her.
- en: We often meet here.
:::

**Statement with another phrase first**

::: {.rule}
fronted phrase + verb + subject + sentence adverb + rest
:::

::: examples
- no: På lørdager møtes vi ofte her.
- en: On Saturdays we often meet here.
:::

**Yes/no question**

::: {.rule}
verb + subject + sentence adverb + rest
:::

::: examples
- no: Møtes dere ofte her?
- en: Do you often meet here?
:::

**Wh-question**

::: {.rule}
question word or phrase + verb + subject + sentence adverb + rest
:::

::: examples
- no: Hvor møtes dere vanligvis?
- en: Where do you usually meet?
:::

Do not reduce the rule to “the verb is always the second word.” Think in sentence elements, and remember that yes/no questions begin with the verb.
