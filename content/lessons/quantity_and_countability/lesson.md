## Dinner for six {#dinner-for-six role=orient}

Sara and Jonas are preparing dinner at Sara’s flat. Two neighbours are coming with their children, so Sara checks what they already have before going to the shop.

::: {.reading translation="How many are coming tonight?" dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Hvor mange kommer i kveld?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Six. Both neighbours are coming, and all their children are coming too." dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Seks. Begge naboene kommer, og alle barna deres blir med.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Do we have enough food?" dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Har vi nok mat?
:::

::: {.reading translation="We have plenty of rice and some tomatoes, but little milk and few carrots." dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Vi har mye ris og noen tomater, men lite melk og få gulrøtter.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then I’ll buy some milk and some carrots." dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Da kjøper jeg litt melk og noen gulrøtter.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Great. I’ll cook all afternoon." dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Flott. Jeg lager mat hele ettermiddagen.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Then everything will be ready before six o’clock." dialogue_id="dinner-for-six" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Da blir alt klart før klokka seks.
:::

The quantity words help Sara and Jonas answer several different questions:

- Can the things be counted individually?
- Are they talking about an amount instead?
- Do they mean every member of a group?
- Do they mean one complete thing or period?
- Is the group made up of exactly two members?

Norwegian uses different words for these meanings. English sometimes hides the difference by using words such as some or all in several situations.

## Things you count and amounts you measure {#things-you-count-and-amounts-you-measure role=model objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

A **countable noun (tellbart substantiv)** refers to things you can normally count individually:

::: examples
- no: én tomat, to tomater, tre tomater
- en: one tomato, two tomatoes, three tomatoes
- no: én gjest, fire gjester
- en: one guest, four guests
:::

An **uncountable noun (utellbart substantiv)** is normally treated as an amount rather than as separate units:

::: examples
- no: ris
- en: rice
- no: melk
- en: milk
- no: tid
- en: time
:::

You can count packets of rice or glasses of milk, but rice and milk themselves are normally treated as amounts. This distinction controls several common quantity words.

**Checkpoint request: identify-count-or-amount**

**Purpose:** Notice the basic distinction before choosing a quantity word.

**Context:** Sara and Jonas’s dinner preparations.

**Learner action:** Sort nouns from the scene according to whether the speaker is counting individual items or describing an amount. Include nouns such as guests, carrots, rice, milk and time.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correctly identify the intended countable or uncountable meaning. A successful response shows that the learner knows whether individual units are being counted in this context.

{{exercise: identify_count_or_amount}}

## Many or much: mange and mye {#many-or-much-mange-and-mye role=model objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

Use **mange** with plural countable nouns. Use **mye** with uncountable amounts.

::: examples
- no: Det kommer mange gjester.
- en: Many guests are coming.
- no: Vi trenger mange stoler.
- en: We need many chairs.
- no: Vi har mye ris.
- en: We have a lot of rice.
- no: Hun drikker mye kaffe.
- en: She drinks a lot of coffee.
:::

The same distinction appears in questions:

::: examples
- no: Hvor mange gjester kommer?
- en: How many guests are coming?
- no: Hvor mye melk trenger vi?
- en: How much milk do we need?
:::

The compact rule is:

- **mange + plural things you can count**
- **mye + an amount you do not normally count directly**

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Hvor mange kopper kaffe lager du?
- en: How many cups of coffee are you making?
- no: Hvor mye kaffe lager du?
- en: How much coffee are you making?
:::

In the first question, the cups are counted. In the second, coffee is treated as an amount.

A common mistake is to choose according to the noun’s physical nature alone. The intended unit matters. Coffee is usually an amount, but cups of coffee are countable. Similarly, bread can be an amount, while loaves or slices are countable.

One useful exception to notice is **penger**, money. Although the Norwegian noun has a plural form, it is normally treated as an amount:

::: examples
- no: De har mye penger.
- en: They have a lot of money.
:::

**Checkpoint request: choose-mange-or-mye**

**Purpose:** Retrieve the central count-versus-amount choice.

**Context:** Jonas sends Sara an updated shopping message containing quantities of food, drinks, plates and guests.

**Learner action:** Complete short Norwegian statements and questions with the appropriate form, using the noun and intended meaning as evidence.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Choose **mange** for countable plural nouns and **mye** for uncountable amounts. A successful response demonstrates control of this contrast without relying on English many or much alone.

{{exercise: choose_mange_or_mye}}

## Some: noen, noe and litt {#some-noen-noe-and-litt role=model objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

When the exact quantity is not important, Norwegian commonly uses **noen** with plural countable nouns.

::: examples
- no: Vi har noen tomater.
- en: We have some tomatoes.
- no: Sara kjøper noen gulrøtter.
- en: Sara buys some carrots.
:::

For an uncountable amount, use **noe** or **litt**.

::: examples
- no: Vi trenger noe melk.
- en: We need some milk.
- no: Det er noe ris i skapet.
- en: There is some rice in the cupboard.
- no: Jeg kjøper litt melk.
- en: I’ll buy a little milk.
- no: Har du litt tid?
- en: Do you have a little time?
:::

**Noe** presents an unspecified amount. **Litt** emphasizes a small amount, often in a neutral or useful way.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Kan du kjøpe noe brød?
- en: Can you buy some bread?
- no: Kan du kjøpe litt brød?
- en: Can you buy a little bread?
:::

Both can be natural. The first asks for an unspecified amount. The second presents the requested amount as small.

Questions often preserve the same count-and-amount distinction:

::: examples
- no: Har vi noen epler?
- en: Do we have any apples?
- no: Har vi noe juice?
- en: Do we have any juice?
:::

Do not use **noe** directly with an ordinary plural countable noun in this meaning.

Incorrect:

::: examples
- no: Vi har noe tomater.
- en: 'Incorrect: We have some tomatoes.'
:::

Natural correction:

::: examples
- no: Vi har noen tomater.
- en: We have some tomatoes.
:::

**Checkpoint request: choose-some-for-count-or-amount**

**Purpose:** Extend the countability decision from large quantities to unspecified quantities.

**Context:** Sara checks cupboards and writes a short shopping note.

**Learner action:** Supply **noen**, **noe** or **litt** in brief descriptions and questions. The contexts should make clear whether the noun is plural and countable, an unspecified amount, or a deliberately small amount.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Choose a natural “some” expression according to countability and intended amount. Other vocabulary serves only as context.

{{exercise: choose_some_for_count_or_amount}}

## Few and little: få, lite and litt {#few-and-little-fa-lite-and-litt role=contrast objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

Use **få** with plural countable nouns when the number is small.

::: examples
- no: Vi har få gulrøtter.
- en: We have few carrots.
- no: Det kom få gjester.
- en: Few guests came.
:::

Use **lite** with uncountable nouns when the amount is small.

::: examples
- no: Vi har lite melk.
- en: We have little milk.
- no: De har lite tid.
- en: They have little time.
:::

Both **få** and **lite** often suggest that the quantity is smaller than wanted or expected. That implication matters.

::: examples
- no: Vi har lite tid.
- en: We have little time.
:::

This can suggest pressure: there may not be enough time.

By contrast, **litt** often presents the small amount more neutrally or positively:

::: examples
- no: Vi har litt tid.
- en: We have a little time.
:::

Here the speaker emphasizes that some time is available.

A similar contrast is possible with countable nouns:

::: examples
- no: Få gjester kom tidlig.
- en: Few guests came early.
- no: Noen få gjester kom tidlig.
- en: A few guests came early.
:::

The first emphasizes the low number. The second presents a small number of guests without necessarily making it sound like a failure.

Compare the dinner situation:

::: examples
- no: Vi har lite melk, så Sara må kjøpe mer.
- en: We have little milk, so Sara has to buy more.
- no: Vi har litt melk til kaffen.
- en: We have a little milk for the coffee.
:::

The amount may be small in both cases. The speaker’s view of that amount differs.

**Checkpoint request: express-small-quantity**

**Purpose:** Practise the grammatical difference between a small number and a small amount.

**Context:** Jonas reviews what remains in the kitchen shortly before the guests arrive.

**Learner action:** Complete descriptions with **få** or **lite**, depending on whether the noun is countable or uncountable.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Match **få** with plural countable nouns and **lite** with amounts. Any suggestion of shortage is supporting context, not a separate scored classification.

{{exercise: express_small_quantity}}

**Checkpoint request: contrast-litt-and-lite**

**Purpose:** Notice how two forms can describe a small amount with different implications.

**Context:** Sara and Jonas discuss whether the remaining time, milk and food are sufficient.

**Learner action:** Choose or produce **litt** when some amount is available and **lite** when the amount is presented as insufficient or worryingly small.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Express the intended positive or negative view of a small uncountable amount. A successful response demonstrates that the learner does not treat **litt** and **lite** as interchangeable.

{{exercise: contrast_litt_and_lite}}

## All members of a group: all, alt and alle {#all-members-of-a-group-all-alt-and-alle role=model objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

Norwegian changes the word corresponding to English all according to the noun.

Use **alle** with plural nouns:

::: examples
- no: Alle gjestene er her.
- en: All the guests are here.
- no: Alle stolene står rundt bordet.
- en: All the chairs are around the table.
:::

Use **all** with a singular common-gender noun, especially an amount:

::: examples
- no: All maten er klar.
- en: All the food is ready.
- no: Hun brukte all melken.
- en: She used all the milk.
:::

Use **alt** with a singular neuter noun:

::: examples
- no: Alt vannet er kaldt.
- en: All the water is cold.
- no: De spiste alt brødet.
- en: They ate all the bread.
:::

**Alt** can also stand alone and mean everything:

::: examples
- no: Alt er klart.
- en: Everything is ready.
- no: Jeg husker alt.
- en: I remember everything.
:::

For a specific plural group, Norwegian commonly uses **alle** followed by the definite plural noun:

::: examples
- no: alle barna
- en: all the children
- no: alle glassene
- en: all the glasses
:::

A common mistake is to use **alt** before every noun because it can mean everything. Its form still has to fit its job.

Compare:

::: examples
- no: Alle koppene er rene.
- en: All the cups are clean.
- no: All kaffen er varm.
- en: All the coffee is hot.
- no: Alt sukkeret er på bordet.
- en: All the sugar is on the table.
:::

**Checkpoint request: choose-all-form**

**Purpose:** Practise the Norwegian forms used for all or everything.

**Context:** The guests have arrived, and Sara checks the table and food.

**Learner action:** Complete descriptions using **all**, **alt** or **alle**, guided by whether the noun is singular common gender, singular neuter or plural. Include some sentences where **alt** stands alone.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Select the form of “all” that agrees with the noun or functions independently as “everything.”

{{exercise: choose_all_form}}

## Every member or one complete thing? {#every-member-or-one-complete-thing role=contrast objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

English all can describe every member of a group or one complete thing. Norwegian normally distinguishes these ideas.

Use **alle** for every member of a plural group:

::: examples
- no: Alle barna spiste.
- en: All the children ate.
- no: Sara vasket alle glassene.
- en: Sara washed all the glasses.
:::

Use **hele** for one complete thing, person, group treated as a unit, or period of time. The noun normally has definite form.

::: examples
- no: Sara vasket hele kjøkkenet.
- en: Sara cleaned the whole kitchen.
- no: Jonas brukte hele ettermiddagen på maten.
- en: Jonas spent the whole afternoon on the food.
- no: Hele familien kom.
- en: The whole family came.
- no: Vi spiste hele kaka.
- en: We ate the whole cake.
:::

The contrast becomes clear when number changes:

::: examples
- no: Hun leste alle bøkene.
- en: She read all the books.
- no: Hun leste hele boka.
- en: She read the whole book.
:::

In the first sentence, several complete members of a group are included. In the second, one book is considered from beginning to end.

With food, **all/alt** and **hele** can create a useful difference:

::: examples
- no: De spiste alt brødet.
- en: They ate all the bread.
- no: De spiste hele brødet.
- en: They ate the whole loaf.
:::

The first focuses on the full available amount. The second treats the bread as one complete item.

Do not normally say **all dagen** for all day. A day is one complete period, so Norwegian uses:

::: examples
- no: hele dagen
- en: all day; the whole day
:::

**Checkpoint request: distinguish-all-members-from-whole-item**

**Purpose:** Separate two meanings that English often expresses with all.

**Context:** Cleaning after dinner involves several plates, one kitchen, several rooms and one long evening.

**Learner action:** Produce or complete sentences using **alle** for every member of a plural group and **hele** for one complete item or period.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Choose between the meaning “every member” and the meaning “one complete whole.” Agreement forms already shown in the sentences are supporting context.

{{exercise: distinguish_all_members_from_whole_item}}

## Exactly two: begge {#exactly-two-begge role=contrast objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

Use **begge** when all members of a group are included and that group contains exactly two.

::: examples
- no: Begge naboene kommer.
- en: Both neighbours are coming.
- no: Begge barna liker ris.
- en: Both children like rice.
- no: Jeg tok med begge flaskene.
- en: I brought both bottles.
:::

**Begge** can also stand alone when the two people or things are already known:

::: examples
- no: Vil Sara og Jonas ha kaffe? Begge vil ha kaffe.
- en: Do Sara and Jonas want coffee? Both want coffee.
:::

Compare **begge** and **alle**:

::: examples
- no: Begge naboene hjelper til.
- en: Both neighbours help.
- no: Alle gjestene hjelper til.
- en: All the guests help.
:::

Use **begge** only when there are two. Use **alle** when every member of a larger or unspecified group is included.

A common error is to use **begge** simply because every person is included. The size of the group matters.

::: examples
- no: Begge foreldrene mine bor i Bergen.
- en: Both my parents live in Bergen.
- no: Alle vennene mine bor i Bergen.
- en: All my friends live in Bergen.
:::

**Checkpoint request: choose-begge-or-alle**

**Purpose:** Connect total inclusion with the size of the group.

**Context:** Sara describes pairs of neighbours, bowls and serving dishes alongside larger groups of guests and plates.

**Learner action:** Complete controlled descriptions with **begge** when the known group has exactly two members and **alle** when the whole plural group is larger or not limited to two.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Choose “both” rather than “all” for an exactly-two group.

{{exercise: choose_begge_or_alle}}

## Back at Sara’s flat {#back-at-saras-flat role=recap objectives=obj-quantity-and-countability-01}

Dinner is almost ready. Sara returns from the shop.

::: {.reading translation="Now we have plenty of milk and many carrots." dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Nå har vi mye melk og mange gulrøtter.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Good. All the guests have arrived." dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Bra. Alle gjestene har kommet.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Are both neighbours here?" dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Er begge naboene her?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Yes, and both are helping. The children have set the whole table." dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Ja, og begge hjelper til. Barna har dekket hele bordet.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Have they used all the glasses?" dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Har de brukt alle glassene?
:::

::: {.reading translation="Almost. There are some glasses left in the kitchen." dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="jonas" speaker_name="Jonas" character_id="jonas"}
Nesten. Det står noen glass igjen på kjøkkenet.
:::

::: {.reading translation="Great. Then everything is ready." dialogue_id="back-at-saras-flat" speaker_id="sara" speaker_name="Sara" character_id="sara"}
Flott. Da er alt klart.
:::

The same quantity choices now perform new jobs. **Mye** and **mange** report what is available. **Alle** includes the full group of guests. **Begge** identifies an exactly-two group. **Hele** treats the table as one complete object. **Noen** refers to an unspecified number of countable glasses, and **alt** sums up the entire situation.

**Checkpoint request: repair-dinner-update**

**Purpose:** Integrate the target forms while staying in the familiar scene.

**Context:** A short written update about the guests, remaining food, two neighbours and the prepared table contains unsuitable quantity words.

**Learner action:** Replace each unsuitable quantity word with a natural one while keeping the intended meaning unchanged.

**Primary scored evidence goal:** Correct quantity-word selection from contextual clues. A successful response shows integrated control across countable things, amounts, all, whole and both.
